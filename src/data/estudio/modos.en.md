## What a mode is

A **mode** is a scale that starts on another degree of a given scale (the **parent** scale). If you play the notes of C major starting on D, you have D Dorian: the same notes, but with D as the centre, and that completely changes the sound.

There are two ways of looking at them, and both are useful:

- **Relative** (same notes): C Ionian, D Dorian, E Phrygian, F Lydian, G Mixolydian, A Aeolian, B Locrian. Useful for **fingerings**: if you know C major in one position, you already know its seven modes.
- **Parallel** (same root): C Lydian, C Ionian, C Mixolydian, C Dorian… Useful for **hearing**: over the same pedal note, each mode has its own colour, and what changes is one or two notes.

For improvising, the parallel view is more useful: don't think "I play C major over Dm7" but "Dm7 → D Dorian: a minor scale with a major 6".

## From bright to dark

The seven modes of the major scale, in parallel, line up from brightest to darkest. Each one lowers **just one note** compared with the previous one:

| Mode | Degrees | Change |
|---|---|---|
| Lydian | 1 2 3 **#4** 5 6 7 | — |
| Ionian | 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 | the 4 drops |
| Mixolydian | 1 2 3 4 5 6 **b7** | the 7 drops |
| Dorian | 1 2 **b3** 4 5 6 b7 | the 3 drops |
| Aeolian | 1 2 b3 4 5 **b6** b7 | the 6 drops |
| Phrygian | 1 **b2** b3 4 5 b6 b7 | the 2 drops |
| Locrian | 1 b2 b3 4 **b5** b6 b7 | the 5 drops |

In the example above they appear in this order in the *Parallel* view. *Play all seven* plays them one after another over the same pedal note: it is the most useful ear exercise on this page.

## The characteristic note

What makes a mode recognisable is the note that separates it from the most similar mode. In the example it is highlighted:

- **Dorian**: the **6** (compared with Aeolian). Without it, it sounds like natural minor.
- **Lydian**: the **#4** (compared with Ionian).
- **Mixolydian**: the **b7** (compared with Ionian).
- **Phrygian**: the **b2** (compared with Aeolian).
- **Locrian**: the **b5** (compared with Phrygian).

When improvising in a mode, **play its characteristic note** and lean on it: if it doesn't sound, the mode isn't heard. Play only the chord tones and nobody will know whether you are in Dorian or Aeolian.

## Melodic minor and harmonic minor

The other two parent scales of jazz give modes with more tension. The most used:

- **Melodic minor** (over mMaj7): the jazz minor, with major 6 and 7.
- **Lydian dominant** (4th mode of melodic minor, over 7#11): the dominant that doesn't resolve, the tritone substitute.
- **Superlocrian or altered** (7th mode of melodic minor, over 7alt): all the altered tensions of the dominant (b9, #9, b5, #5).
- **Locrian #2** (6th mode of melodic minor, over m7b5): the half-diminished with a natural 9.
- **Phrygian dominant** (5th mode of harmonic minor, over 7b9): the V7 of the minor ii–V–i, and the sound of flamenco.

Choose *Melodic minor* or *Harmonic minor* in the example to hear them all.

## How to practise them

1. **Ear first.** *Play all seven* in parallel, with a sustained sound. Sing each one's characteristic note over the pedal note.
2. **One mode, one week.** Choose one, loop it and improvise over it with just the root as a pedal. Look for phrases that make its characteristic note sound.
3. **Parallel modes on the guitar.** In one position, play C Ionian and then C Mixolydian, C Dorian… without moving the hand. Notice which finger changes (the neck map in [Scales](../escalas/) helps).
4. **Relative modes for fingering.** Play the major scale in one position; now start and end on each degree (D Dorian, E Phrygian…) without changing the fingering.
5. **Chord → mode.** For each chord of a standard, say which mode you would use: m7 → Dorian, 7 → Mixolydian (or Lydian dominant, altered…), maj7 → Ionian or Lydian, m7b5 → Locrian or Locrian #2.
6. **Modal vamps.** One chord for a long time (Dm7 in *So What*, for example) is the best place to practise a mode: there is no rush to change scale.

## Ideas for variety

- **Same lick, different mode.** Play a phrase in C Dorian and change it to C Aeolian by changing only the 6. Then to C Phrygian (the 2 changes too).
- **Triads of the mode.** In each mode, find the major triad that contains its characteristic note (in D Dorian, the G triad; in F Lydian, the G one): playing it over the pedal makes the mode sound instantly.
- **Pedal and modes on the neck.** Use the open low string as a pedal (E, A or D) and go through the parallel modes on the high strings.
