## The idea

Bebop is a language of **eighth notes** that outlines the harmony with a single line. Its main rule is simple: **chord tones go on the beats** and in between go passing notes, chromaticism and enclosures. That way, even though the line uses many notes outside the chord, the ear hears the chords: the strong beats state them and the rest is the path from one chord tone to the next.

This page brings together three tools for achieving it:

- **The bebop scale:** an eight-note scale that, played stepwise in eighths, leaves the chord tones on the beats by themselves.
- **Chained enclosures:** surrounding each chord tone from above and below before playing it (see [Chromatic approaches](../aproximaciones/)).
- **Stock phrases:** formulas that recur in every solo of the era, especially over the ii–V.

The example above generates lines over any progression on the site with each tool. Chord tones are filled in: notice that they always fall on the beats.

## The bebop scale

A seven-note scale played in eighths doesn't fit the bar: if you start on the root on beat 1, the 7 falls off the beat, the 6 on beat 2 and things get out of order. With **eight notes** it fits: an octave is exactly two bars of 2/4, and if the chord tones are in alternate positions, they all fall on the beats.

To do that, a **chromatic passing note** is added to the chord's scale:

| Scale | Notes in C | Passing note | Notes on the beats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dominant bebop | C D E F G A Bb **B** | 7 between the b7 and the root | C7: C E G Bb |
| Major bebop | C D E F G **G#** A B | #5 between the 5 and the 6 | C6: C E G A |
| Minor bebop | C D Eb F G A Bb **B** | 7 between the b7 and the root | Cm7: C Eb G Bb |
| Melodic minor bebop | C D Eb F G **G#** A B | #5 between the 5 and the 6 | Cm6: C Eb G A |
| Locrian bebop | C Db Eb F Gb Ab Bb **B** | 7 between the b7 and the root | Cø7: C Eb Gb Bb |
| Diminished | C D Eb F Gb G# A B | already has eight notes | C°7: C Eb Gb A |

How it is used:

- **Start on a chord tone on a beat** and continue stepwise. Descending is the most common use; ascending works just as well.
- **Over maj7, think 6.** Major bebop puts the 6 on the beat, not the 7: it is the sound of Charlie Parker and Bud Powell over major chords. The 7 still appears off the beat.
- **If you change direction or leap**, the note that falls on the beat must still be a chord tone. If it isn't, add a chromatic note or leap to another chord tone to realign the line.
- **In the ii–V you can think everything as dominant.** Dm7 – G7 share the notes of G's dominant bebop scale starting elsewhere: many boppers play the V's scale over both chords.

## Chained enclosures

An **enclosure** surrounds the target note: one note above (usually the scale note) and one below (usually a half step), and then the target on the beat. Chaining them means moving from one target to the next like this:

1. **Target** (chord tone) on beat 1.
2. **A scale step** off the beat, towards where the next target is.
3. **Enclosure** of the next target on beat 2 and its off-beat.
4. **Next target** on beat 3, and start again.

The targets form a slow line (half notes) of chord tones; the enclosures decorate it. The better chosen the targets (the guide tones, the 3 and the 7, which move little), the clearer the chord change sounds.

## Stock phrases over the ii–V

Some formulas appear in every bebop solo. They are worth learning as vocabulary, in every key:

- **3 to 9:** arpeggio up from the 3 (3–5–7–9) and down the bebop scale from the root. Over Dm7: F A C E | D C# C B. It is the example of the "3 to 9" tool.
- **3 to b9 on the dominant:** over G7 going to C, B D F Ab. The b9 (Ab) resolves a half step down to the 5 of the next chord (G) or keeps descending through the scale.
- **Enclosing the 3 of the next chord:** at the end of the ii–V, enclose the 3 of the tonic (E in C: F – D# – E) to land on it on beat 1. It is the most typical resolution.
- **The ii's arpeggio over the V:** playing the Dm7 (or Fmaj7) arpeggio over G7 gives the 9, 11 and 13 tensions without thinking about them.

## How to practise it

1. **Bebop scale on one chord.** With a G7 in the background, descend the dominant bebop scale from each chord tone (G, F, D and B), in eighths, checking that the beats fall on G B D F. Repeat in every key.
2. **Go through the ii–V–I with the scale.** "Bebop scale" tool, one bar per chord, on a loop at a slow tempo. Play what comes out and then make up your own lines with the same rules.
3. **Stock phrases in twelve keys.** Choose "3 to 9", learn the ii–V–I line and transpose it with the key selector. First in one position, then in another.
4. **Enclosures in half notes.** Play only the targets (one chord tone every two beats), then add the enclosures. That is the "Chained enclosures" tool.
5. **Sing before you play.** Bebop has accents and articulation: off-beat notes tied to the next and accents on the chromatic notes. Sing the line with swing before playing it.

## Ideas for variety

- **Change tool on each chord.** That is what "Mix" does: an arpeggio on one, the scale on the next and an enclosure to enter the new chord.
- **Start off the beat.** Shift the line by one eighth and find another way for the chord tones to fall on the beats (an extra chromatic note realigns it).
- **Use the lines in the repertoire.** Every ii–V in a standard is a place for a stock phrase: find the ii–Vs in *Stella by Starlight* or *Days of Wine and Roses* and try the example's lines there.
- **Combine with guide tones.** Make the first beat of each chord its 3 or its 7 (see [Guide tones](../notas-guia/)): bebop lines sound clearer when they land on them.
