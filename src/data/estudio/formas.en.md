## The idea

The **form** of a tune is its map: how many bars it has and how they are divided into sections that repeat or contrast. In jazz the whole form is played over and over: the melody is the first **chorus**, each solo takes one or more choruses and at the end the melody comes back. Nobody says "three bars to go before the bridge": every musician is expected to know.

Knowing the form is useful for three things:

- **Not getting lost.** If you get lost in a solo, the form brings you back: in an AABA the bridge always arrives at bar 17.
- **Learning faster.** A 32-bar AABA is really 16 different bars: the A (eight) and the bridge (eight). The rest repeats.
- **Building the solo.** A good improvisation has a form too: raise the tension in the bridge, breathe at the end of each section and reach the last A with something to wrap up.

Sections are named with letters: the first idea is **A**, the next different one is **B**, and so on. A section that repeats almost the same takes the same letter with a prime (**A′**). Almost all of them last **eight bars**, and within each one the phrases usually go in **fours** (or twos).

## AABA: song form

The form of the Broadway musicals of the 1920s to 50s, where most standards come from. Thirty-two bars: **A A B A**.

- **First A (1–8):** presents the melody. It usually ends in a **turnaround** (IIm7–V7, or I–VI–II–V) that leads back to the start.
- **Second A (9–16):** the same melody; the ending changes to close on the tonic. In charts it is written once with **two endings** (1st and 2nd ending).
- **B, the bridge (17–24):** contrast. Another harmonic area (the IV, the III, a key a 3rd away…), another melody and, at the end, a dominant that prepares the return. It is where it is easiest to get lost: it arrives just when the form seems predictable.
- **Last A (25–32):** back home. Sometimes with an extended ending (tag) on the last chorus.

To hear it, count out loud "one, two, three, four" and sing the melody inside your head: after two identical As, something changes. Tunes: *Satin Doll*, *Take the A Train*, *Honeysuckle Rose*, *Misty*, *Body and Soul*, *'Round Midnight*. There are none in the Repertoire yet; the example above is a model with the typical harmony.

## ABAC: two halves that start the same

Thirty-two bars in two halves of 16. Both start with the same A; the first ends open (B, usually on the dominant or in another key) and the second closes (C). It sounds like a question and its answer, on a large scale.

- **A (1–8) and A (17–24):** the same. When you hear the beginning come back, you know you are at bar 17.
- **B (9–16):** the first ending. It usually touches VIm or IIm with their ii–Vs and stays on the dominant.
- **C (25–32):** the second ending, with the cadence to the tonic and the turnaround for the next chorus.

In the Repertoire: [Days of Wine and Roses](../../repertorio/days-of-wine-and-roses/) (the example above). Also *All of Me*, *But Not for Me*, *On Green Dolphin Street*, *There Will Never Be Another You*.

## Blues: three four-bar phrases

Twelve bars in **three phrases of four**: tonic (1–4), subdominant (5–8) and dominant with turnaround (9–12). The lyrics follow the same scheme, **A A B**: a line, the same line over the IV and the answer. That is why the blues can be written with letters like the other forms.

The form does not change even when the harmony gets more complex: a jazz blues or a Bird blues has the same 12 bars and the same three phrases (see [Blues](../blues/), with the variants bar by bar).

In the Repertoire: [Turnaround](../../repertorio/turnaround/) (a blues in C with a descending line of minor chords in bars 9 and 10) and [Footprints](../../repertorio/footprints/), a **24-bar minor blues in 3/4**: each 6/4 bar of the 12-bar blues, split in two. Counted in 3/4 it seems strange; counted in sixes, it is a blues.

## Rhythm changes

The chords of *I Got Rhythm* (George Gershwin, 1930). Bebop musicians wrote dozens of new melodies on them (*Oleo*, *Anthropology*, *Moose the Mooche*, *Rhythm-a-ning*), and along with the blues it is the form most played at jam sessions. Almost always in **Bb** and fast.

- **The As:** I–VI–II–V turnarounds with two chords per bar. In bars 5–6, I7 going to IV and a passing diminished chord (or IVm). First and second endings as in any AABA.
- **The bridge:** four dominants by fifths, **two bars each**: III7 – VI7 – II7 – V7 (in Bb: D7 – G7 – C7 – F7). The harmonic rhythm changes: from two chords per bar to one every two bars.

When improvising, the As can be played as "all Bb" (Bb scale and blues) or making every change; the bridge calls for marking each dominant (guide tones, see [Guide tones](../notas-guia/)). The bars are also in [Progressions](../../progresiones/) (Rhythm changes A and bridge).

## Other forms

Many tunes, especially from the 1960s on, do not fit the moulds:

- **16 bars:** [Cantaloupe Island](../../repertorio/cantaloupe-island/) is four chords of four bars each (A B C A). *Blue Bossa* is AB, eight and eight. With so few changes, knowing where you are is what gives the solo its meaning.
- **AA′:** [E.S.P.](../../repertorio/esp/) is two halves of 16 that only differ in the last four bars. Like ABAC, but the middle part repeats too.
- **Through-composed:** [Stella by Starlight](../../repertorio/stella-by-starlight/) repeats no section in its 32 bars. There are no shortcuts: you have to learn all of it.
- **Irregular forms:** [Infant Eyes](../../repertorio/infant-eyes/) has 27 bars; *Solar*, 12 without being a blues. There are tunes with sections of 10, of 6 or with an extra bar: count them, do not assume.
- **AABC and other variants:** *Autumn Leaves* repeats the A and then has two different sections.

## Intros, endings and codas

The form is the chorus; what comes before and after is separate.

- **Intro:** often a four- or eight-bar turnaround over the end of the form (I–VI–II–V, or the last bars of the tune). When the intro ends, bar 1 begins.
- **First and second endings:** the same section written once with two endings. In an AABA, the 1st ending is the end of the first A and the 2nd that of the second.
- **Final turnaround:** the last two bars of the form are usually a turnaround that only serves to go back to the top. On the last chorus it is replaced by the tonic or an ending.
- **Tag:** repeating the last four bars (or the last ii–V) two or three times before ending.
- **Coda:** an ending written separately, jumped to from a point in the last time through.
- **Vamp:** a couple of chords repeated with no fixed number of bars, for an intro, an ending or an open solo. You leave it on a cue.

## How not to get lost

- **Count the bars** at first, out loud or on your fingers: "one-two-three-four, two-two-three-four…". In time you count only by four-bar phrases.
- **Sing the melody in your head** while you improvise or comp. It is what almost every jazz musician does: the melody is the map.
- **Mark the section changes.** When you reach the bridge, change something: register, rhythm, density. If you mark it yourself, you won't skip it.
- **Listen to the drummer.** Drummers usually play a fill or a cymbal crash at the end of each eight-bar section and at the end of the chorus.
- **If you get lost,** don't stop: play little (long notes, guide tones) and listen to the bass until you recognise the return to A or the turnaround. At the start of the next chorus you are back in.

## Exercises

1. **Listen without looking.** Choose a form above, turn on "Hide chords" and turn off "Follow along". Play it and say each section out loud as it starts ("A, A, bridge, A"). Check with the marker turned on.
2. **Choruses in a row.** With "⟲ Choruses in a row" and the metronome, play only the root of each chord for three choruses without losing count. Then only guide tones (3 and 7).
3. **One note per section.** Improvise with a single note on each A and a different one on the bridge: that way the form is heard in your solo.
4. **From the Repertoire.** For each tune you study, write out its form (letters and bars) before learning the melody. When a tune fits no mould, count the sections as they are.
5. **In other keys.** Play the map of a form (rhythm changes, for example) in another key with the selector above. The form does not change; the chords do.
