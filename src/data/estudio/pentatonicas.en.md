## The idea

The pentatonic is the scale almost every guitarist learns first: five notes, no half steps, a comfortable shape on the neck. In jazz it is used differently: not only **the pentatonic on the root**, but **pentatonics that start on other notes of the chord**, chosen so that their five notes are chord tones and tensions (9, 11, 13 or altered).

Having no half steps, a well-chosen pentatonic has no notes that clash hard with the chord. And being only five notes with leaps of a 3rd, it sounds open and modern: the sound of McCoy Tyner, Woody Shaw or John Coltrane in his modal period. The same old fingering, moved to another fret, changes the colour without learning anything new.

In the example above you choose the chord and you will see which pentatonics work, which notes each one has as degrees of the chord (tensions highlighted) and where they are on the neck, with the chord tones filled in.

## Which pentatonic over each chord

In C (change the key in the example to see them in another):

| Chord | Pentatonic | Degrees over the chord | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cmaj7 | C major | 1 9 3 5 13 | inside, 6/9 |
| Cmaj7 | G major | 5 13 7 9 3 | maj9 |
| Cmaj7(#11) | D major | 9 3 #11 13 7 | Lydian |
| Cm7 | C minor | 1 b3 11 5 b7 | blues, rock |
| Cm7 | G minor | 5 b7 1 9 11 | open |
| Cm7 | D minor | 9 11 5 13 1 | Dorian, modal |
| C7 | C major | 1 9 3 5 13 | swing, country |
| C7 | C minor | 1 #9 11 5 b7 | blues |
| C7 | G minor | 5 b7 1 9 11 | suspended |
| C7sus4 | Bb major | b7 1 9 11 5 | sus |
| C7sus4 | F major | 11 5 13 1 9 | sus with 13 |
| C7alt | Gb major (= Eb minor) | b5 b13 b7 b9 #9 | altered |
| Cø7 | Eb minor | b3 b5 b13 b7 b9 | Locrian |
| Cø7 | F minor | 11 b13 b7 1 b3 | softer |

Two rules that sum up almost the whole table:

- **Major from the 1, the 5 and the 9.** Over a major chord, the major pentatonics on the root, the fifth and the ninth sound further and further away (from inside to Lydian), but never clash.
- **Minor from the 1, the 5 and the 9.** Over a minor chord, the same with the minor pentatonics.

Remember the **relatives**: C major and A minor are the same five notes. "G major over Cmaj7" is the same as "E minor over Cmaj7"; use whichever name is easier for you to see on the neck.

## In the ii–V–I

A very practical way to use them in a ii–V–I in C:

- **Dm7:** D minor (inside) or A minor (from the 5).
- **G7:** G major (inside), D minor (suspended, the same as on Dm7) or Db major (altered, which resolves very well to C).
- **Cmaj7:** C major, G major or D major.

**One pentatonic for the whole ii–V:** A minor (= C major) works over Dm7, G7 and Cmaj7. It is the simplest way to start, although it doesn't mark the changes; over G7, its C is the 11 (it clashes with the 3, B): use it in passing, not as a long note. To mark the changes, switch to the altered one on the V7, the major pentatonic on its b5 (Db major over G7), and resolve to the tonic's. That jump from "outside" to "inside" is the sound of the modern ii–V.

## How to practise it

1. **One pentatonic, five positions.** Choose the root pentatonic, "Position" area, and go up the neck changing "From fret" position by position. Notice where the chord tones fall (filled in).
2. **Change root, not shape.** With the chord fixed and the background chord sounding, play the same fingering from the 1, the 5 and the 9. Listen to how the colour changes with the same shape.
3. **Groups of 4.** Pentatonics sound great in groups of four notes (the "Groups of 4" figure): the typical pattern of Coltrane and McCoy Tyner in modal tunes.
4. **Inside and outside.** On a dominant chord, alternate two bars of the root's major pentatonic and two of the major on the b5 (altered). Always end on a chord tone.
5. **Apply it in a tune.** *Cantaloupe Island* and *Footprints* have long chords where you can try the three pentatonics of each chord.

## Ideas for variety

- **Pentatonic with a passing note:** add a chromatic note between two of its notes (as in the [bebop scale](../bebop/)) so the chord tones fall on the beats.
- **Leaps of a fourth:** play the pentatonic skipping every other note: you get fourths, the sound of quartal chords.
- **Pentatonics in motifs:** take a three-note motif from the pentatonic and transpose it to the one on the 5 and the 9 (see [Motifs and development](../motivos/)).
- **Chaining through chords:** in a progression, change pentatonic on each chord keeping the same position on the neck: it forces you to find the nearest note.
