## The idea

**Comping** (from *accompanying*, or from *complementing*) is accompanying another musician: playing the chords so the music moves forward and the soloist has something to lean on, without getting in their way. In a jazz group, the comping guitarist decides three things in every bar:

- **The rhythm:** when the chord sounds (on 1, off the beat, anticipated…).
- **The voicing:** which notes and how many (three-note shells, four-note drop 2).
- **The register:** which area of the neck (low, middle or high).

Good comping combines all three and varies them, but without drawing attention: the soloist is the star.

In the example above you choose a progression and it generates an accompaniment: one rhythmic cell per bar (fixed or varied), drop 2 or shell voicings that move as little as possible, and a register that is fixed or changes every four bars. You can add bass and hi-hat to hear it as in a band.

## The rhythm

The cells in the example are the basic swing ones (see [Rhythm](../ritmo/)):

- **Quarter notes (Freddie Green):** a short hit on every beat. The basis of big-band swing; with an upright bass, the guitar is hardly heard, it is felt.
- **Charleston:** beat 1 and the "&" of 2. The most used comping rhythm.
- **Reverse Charleston:** the "&" of 1 and beat 3. More suspended.
- **On 2 and 4:** short hits with the drummer's hi-hat.
- **Whole note:** a long chord on 1. It leaves all the space; ideal in ballads or when the soloist plays a lot.
- **Anticipation and syncopation:** the next chord arrives early, on the "&" of 4 (or of 2, if the chord changes mid-bar). It is what makes swing push forward.

**Vary, but with logic.** Repeating the same cell all the time is boring; changing it every bar with no criterion sounds nervous. A good rule: four-bar phrases, with an anticipation into the next one in the last bar. That is what the "Varied" option does.

## Voicings and register

- **Few notes.** Three (shells) or four (drop 2) are enough. The more notes, the more space you take up.
- **Voice leading.** Between one chord and the next, move the hand as little as possible (see [Drop 2](../drop2/) and [Shell voicings](../shells/)): the listener hears lines, not jumps.
- **Below the soloist.** If the soloist plays high, comp in the middle or low register, and vice versa. Two instruments in the same register cover each other.
- **Changing register** every four or eight bars is a simple way to add variety without changing anything else (the "Alternate" option).

## Listening to the soloist

Comping cannot be fully prepared: it is a conversation.

- **Leave space.** When the soloist plays long, dense phrases, play less (whole notes, isolated hits).
- **Fill the gaps.** When the soloist breathes, a couple of hits answer their phrase.
- **Follow the intensity.** If the solo builds, the comping builds with it (more hits, higher register); if it drops, so does the comping.
- **Do not compete with the piano.** If there is a piano, one of you stops comping or both play very little.

## How to practise it

1. **One cell, one progression.** With the example on "Fixed", play the Charleston on a ii–V–I with bass and hi-hat until it comes by itself. Then each cell.
2. **Varied.** Set "Varied" and play what you hear; then make up your own variations with the same cells.
3. **Register.** Play the same progression in low, middle and high drop 2. Then "Alternate".
4. **With a soloist.** Record a melody or a solo (or use a recording) and comp behind it: less when it plays, more when it breathes.
5. **A blues.** The jazz blues is the best progression for practising comping: twelve bars, many chords and a short form.

## Ideas for variety

- **Single notes instead of chords:** sometimes the 3 and the 7 are enough (see [Guide tones](../notas-guia/)).
- **Bossa rhythms:** with the bossa nova rhythm and middle-register voicings, the same example works for Latin tunes (see the bossa clave in Rhythm).
- **Quartal chords:** in modal tunes, comp with [fourths](../cuartas/) and the same rhythm.
- **Comping and melody:** in a guitar solo, alternate phrases and chord hits (the style of Wes Montgomery or Jim Hall).
