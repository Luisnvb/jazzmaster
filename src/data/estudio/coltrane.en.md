## The idea

In 1959, John Coltrane recorded *Giant Steps*, a tune that changes key every two beats. It doesn't do so at random: it goes through **three keys a major third apart** (in the original, B, G and Eb), and reaches each one through its dominant. Since three major thirds add up to an octave, the cycle **divides the octave into three equal parts** and returns to the start.

This idea is called **Coltrane changes**. Besides *Giant Steps*, Coltrane used it to reharmonise standards: *Countdown* is *Tune Up* with this cycle inserted into each ii–V–I, and *26-2* does the same with *Confirmation*.

In the example above you choose the progression and see, for each chord, which key it belongs to, its guide tones and a shell voicing on the neck. The cycle of fifths marks the three centres (they form a triangle) and, as it plays, the centre you are in.

## The cycle of major thirds

In C, the three centres are **C, Ab and E** (the notes of an augmented triad). Each is reached through its dominant:

| Chord | Role | Key |
|---|---|---|
| Cmaj7 | I | C |
| Eb7 | V7 | Ab |
| Abmaj7 | I | Ab |
| B7 | V7 | E |
| Emaj7 | I | E |
| G7 | V7 | C |
| Cmaj7 | I | C |

It is the example's "Cycle of major thirds" progression. Notice the movement: each centre **falls a major 3rd** (C → Ab → E → C), and each dominant is a minor 3rd above the centre it leaves (Eb7 after C).

Since there are only four different augmented triads (C-E-Ab, Db-F-A, D-F#-Bb, Eb-G-B), there are only four cycles: learn the four and you have them all.

## The Coltrane ii–V–I

The most useful form in practice is inserting the cycle **between the IIm7 and the I** of an ordinary ii–V–I:

- Ordinary: Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 | Cmaj7
- With Coltrane changes: Dm7 Eb7 | Abmaj7 B7 | Emaj7 G7 | Cmaj7

It starts and ends the same way (Dm7 and Cmaj7, with G7 just before), so it fits any standard with a four-bar ii–V–I. That is what Coltrane did in *Countdown*.

## Giant Steps

The 16 bars of *Giant Steps* combine the cycle with ii–V–Is: the first half goes down the cycle (B, G, Eb) with a dominant between centres; the second half goes up through ii–V–Is (Am7–D7 → G, C#m7–F#7 → B, Fm7–Bb7 → Eb). Each chord lasts one or two beats and the original tempo is fast, which is why it is a benchmark tune for practice.

On this site, Giant Steps is written with the tonic on the first chord: with "Key B" you see it in the original key.

## Guide tones and shells

Even though the keys jump, **the guide tones move little**: almost always a half or whole step, as in a ii–V–I. It is the best way to understand (and play) the changes: if each chord's 3 and 7 are clear, everything else leans on them.

With shell voicings (root, 3 and 7), each chord change moves the hand as little as possible: the example chooses the shape closest to the previous one. Play the shells at a slow tempo first until your hand finds them by itself.

## How to practise it

1. **Roots.** Play only the root of each chord, in quarter notes, on a loop. Then sing the centres (C, Ab, E) as it plays.
2. **Guide tones.** Play the two guide-tone lines in the example; then only the top one, as if it were a melody.
3. **Shells.** Play the example's shells at a slow tempo (60–80) and speed up little by little.
4. **1-2-3-5 arpeggios.** Over each chord, the four notes 1, 2, 3 and 5 (on an m7, 1-2-b3-5): the pattern Coltrane himself used to play over these changes.
5. **The four cycles.** Change the key to C, Db, D and Eb: they are the four possible cycles.

## Ideas for variety

- **Put it in a standard:** find a four-bar ii–V–I (for example in *Days of Wine and Roses*) and play the Coltrane ii–V–I over it.
- **Over a fixed chord:** over a Cmaj7 vamp, improvise going round the cycle (C, Ab, E) as if it were an excursion outside (see [Outside and inside](../outside/)).
- **With pentatonics:** one major pentatonic per centre (C, Ab, E) is the simplest way to sound inside (see [Pentatonics in jazz](../pentatonicas/)).
- **With bebop lines:** the [bebop line generator](../bebop/) accepts these progressions: choose "Giant Steps" and see how it chains the scales.
