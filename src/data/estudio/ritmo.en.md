## The pulse, inside you

Rhythm is not practised to play "in time" with the metronome, but to **carry the pulse inside you** so that the metronome only confirms it. That is why it pays to take work away from it bit by bit:

1. Click on **every beat**.
2. Click only on **2 and 4**, like the drummer's hi-hat in swing. You supply 1 and 3.
3. Click only on **beat 1** of each bar.
4. Click **every two bars** (set the tempo to half and think of each click as beat 1 of a two-beat bar): you carry the rest.

In the grid above, the *Metronome* selector has the first three steps.

## Subdivisions

Each beat can be split into two (**eighth notes**), three (**triplets**) or four (**sixteenth notes**). Practising subdivisions out loud puts everything else in order:

| Division | Counted | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Eighths | 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & | swing, bossa, rock |
| Triplets | 1 trip-let 2 trip-let… | shuffle, slow blues, 12/8 |
| Sixteenths | 1 e & a 2 e & a… | funk, latin, double-time ballads |

Classic exercise: with the metronome fixed, play one note in quarter notes, then in eighths, triplets and sixteenths, four bars each, and back. The change between triplets and sixteenths is the hardest.

## Swing

In jazz, eighth notes are not even: the one on the beat is longer and the off-beat one is shorter and slightly accented. It is roughly a **triplet** (long-short, 2+1), but not exactly:

- **At slow tempos**, the swing is stronger (close to the triplet or beyond).
- **At fast tempos**, the eighths get closer to being even.

The *Swing* control above goes from straight eighths (50 %) to triplet (67 %) and a bit beyond. Try the same rhythm with several values and listen for the one that "walks".

## Comping rhythms

The patterns above are the most used for comping:

- **Quarter notes (Freddie Green)**: a short hit on every beat, with no accents. The basis of big-band swing; with *Short hits* turned on.
- **Charleston** (1 and the "&" of 2) and its **reverse** (the "&" of 1 and 3): the two rhythms most standards are comped with.
- **Anticipations**: the new chord arrives on the "&" of 4 of the previous bar. It gives the changes forward motion.
- **Claves**, son (3-2 and 2-3) and **bossa nova**: the rhythmic skeleton of Latin and Brazilian music. The accompaniment is organised around them even when they are not played.

What matters is not always playing the same pattern, but **combining** several and leaving space: comping is a conversation with the soloist.

## How to practise it

1. **Count out loud.** Before playing, count the subdivisions of the pattern ("1 & 2 &…") and clap on the hits. Then play it with a single chord.
2. **Metronome on 2 and 4.** Every pattern with the click only on 2 and 4. When that works, only on 1.
3. **Make your own patterns.** Press the cells of the grid to remove and add hits. Try two-bar rhythms that answer each other.
4. **Displacement.** Take a pattern and start it an eighth note later (or earlier). The same rhythm in another place in the bar sounds completely different.
5. **3 over 4.** The *3 over 4* pattern puts a hit every three eighths: three bars until it lines up with beat 1 again. Play it until you can feel both pulses at once.
6. **Rhythm before notes.** Choose a pattern and use it to improvise: a single note first, then the chord tones, then the scale, but **always with that rhythm**.
7. **In the repertoire.** Comp a standard changing pattern every four bars: quarter notes, Charleston, anticipations… With [shell voicings](../shells/), the rhythm comes through even more clearly.

## Ideas for variety

- **Rests.** Play a pattern and remove one hit each time round until only one is left: the time has to stay just as steady.
- **Double time and half time.** The same pattern feeling the pulse at double or half speed, without changing the metronome.
- **Accents.** Play continuous eighths and accent only the off-beats; then only one in three (the 3 over 4 again).
- **With your voice.** Sing the rhythmic phrase with syllables ("doo-ba-doo-BA") before playing it: that is how swing sounds.
