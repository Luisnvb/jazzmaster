## The idea

An **approach** is a note (or a group of notes) that leads to a **target note**: almost always a chord tone that falls on a strong beat. The approach notes go on the weak beat or off the beat and create tension; the target note resolves it.

It is the basis of the bebop language: phrases sound "inside" the harmony even though they use notes that are not in the chord, because those notes always land on a chord tone at the right moment. What you hear is the **target**; the approach is the path.

Three rules:

- **The target on the strong beat** (1 and 3 in 4/4), or on beat 1 of the next chord when the harmony changes.
- **The approach on the weak beat** (2 and 4) or on the off-beat eighth ("&").
- **By half step or by scale step.** A note a half step from a chord tone always sounds good if it resolves. The scale note is softer.

## Types of approach

Examples towards E (the 3 of Cmaj7):

| Pattern | Notes | How it sounds |
|---|---|---|
| Chromatic from below | D# → **E** | the most common, pushes upwards |
| Chromatic from above | F → **E** | more tense, typical on 3s and 7s |
| From the scale, above | F → **E** | here it coincides with the chromatic one; on other notes it is a whole step |
| Double chromatic from below | D D# → **E** | two half steps, takes two eighths |
| Double chromatic from above | Gb F → **E** | the same from above |
| Enclosure | F D# → **E** | surrounds the target: above (scale) and below (half step) |
| Chromatic enclosure | F D# → **E** | above and below by half step (here the same as the previous one) |
| Reverse enclosure | D# F → **E** | below first, then above |
| Long enclosure | F D D# → **E** | scale above, scale below and a half step below |
| Triple chromatic from below | C# D D# → **E** | three half steps: a very bebop sound |

**Enclosures** are the most characteristic: surrounding the target note with one note above and one below before landing on it. Charlie Parker used them constantly.

## Towards which notes

- **Guide tones (3 and 7).** They define the chord: approaching them at each change draws the harmony very clearly. In a progression, the example above uses the top [guide-tone](../notas-guia/) line as targets.
- **The root and the 5.** More stable, good for ending a phrase.
- **Tensions (9, 13).** For more modern phrases: the approach makes them sound intentional.

## How to practise them

1. **One chord and one pattern.** Choose a chord and a pattern in the example, on a loop. Play the four chord tones (1, 3, 5, 7) as targets, one per bar. Sing before you play.
2. **On the guitar.** Find each approach in the position from the [routine](../rutina/): the chromatic ones are almost always on the next fret, on the same string. Then on the neighbouring string.
3. **Every pattern towards one note.** Fix a target (the 3, for example) and go through the ten patterns. Choose two you like and stick with them for a week.
4. **In a progression.** Switch to *Progression* in the example: each chord's target is its top guide tone. Play only the approaches and the target; leave the rest of the bar silent.
5. **Fill the rest.** Now improvise in the remaining space, but always reach the target with the approach on the last beat.
6. **Move the target.** Land on beat 3 instead of 1, or anticipate the target on the "&" of 4 of the previous bar.
7. **In your tunes.** Over a standard from the repertoire, one approach per bar towards the 3 of the new chord. Then towards the 7.

## Ideas for variety

- **Arpeggio with approaches.** Go up the arpeggio (1, 3, 5, 7) putting a half step below each note: C#-D, D#-E, F#-G, A#-B…
- **Bebop scale.** The Mixolydian scale with the major 7 added as a passing note (C D E F G A Bb B): that way, in eighths, the chord tones fall on the strong beats.
- **Question and answer.** Play a phrase that ends on a target with an enclosure; answer with another that ends on the next target.
- **Only two notes.** Improvise using only one approach and its target on each chord, changing pattern every bar.
