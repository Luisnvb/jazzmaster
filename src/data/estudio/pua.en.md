## The idea

The left hand decides which notes sound; the right hand decides **how** they sound: the attack, the rhythm and, above all, the ease at fast tempos. In jazz guitar with a pick (Wes Montgomery played with his thumb, but Joe Pass, Pat Martino and George Benson use a pick) there are three ways to move the pick, and each has its own logic:

- **Alternate picking:** down, up, down, up, always, whether or not you change string.
- **Economy picking:** alternate within each string, but when changing string the pick keeps going in the direction it was already moving.
- **Sweep picking:** economy taken to the extreme, with one note per string: a single motion of the pick that "sweeps" across several strings in a row.

## Alternate picking

It is the foundation. The hand moves like a steady pendulum: **downstrokes fall on the beats and strong parts**, upstrokes on the off-beats. That fixed relationship between hand and rhythm is its great advantage: swing comes from the hand, because the upstroke is naturally softer.

Its difficulty is **string changes**. When the pick finishes one string and moves to the next, one of two things can happen:

- **Inside picking:** the pick attacks both strings from the gap between them (up on the upper string and down on the lower one). It stays "trapped" between the two.
- **Outside picking:** it attacks both from outside, and has to jump over a string to reach the other.

Many people are comfortable with one and not the other. With **two or four notes per string** the changes are always the same kind (look at the pentatonic and the chromatic exercise); with **three**, or with a scale in position that mixes two and three, they alternate. It is worth practising both.

## Economy picking

Economy is alternate picking **as long as you do not change string**. When moving to another string the pick does not go back: if it was going down and the new string is below (towards the 1st), it keeps going down and plays it on the way. Moving towards the 6th, the same with upstrokes.

- **Advantage:** you save the outside jumps; with three notes per string the hand makes a very fluid motion (down-up-down, down-up-down… going up).
- **Drawback:** the stroke is no longer tied to the rhythm. In three-notes-per-string scales in eighths, the strong beats sometimes fall on downstrokes and sometimes on upstrokes, and the accent shifts. That is why in jazz it is used more for fast lines than for phrases with a lot of swing.

It is Frank Gambale's technique, and more loosely that of many jazz guitarists who use alternate picking "with exceptions" where economy comes naturally.

## Sweep picking

When each note is on a different string (an arpeggio), economy becomes a **sweep**: the pick does not strike each string, it **falls** through them in a single motion, muting each note as the next one sounds so they do not ring together like a chord.

The keys:

- **One motion**, not several strokes in a row: the pick pushes the string, rests on the next one and carries on.
- **Each note is muted** by lifting the left-hand finger as soon as the next one sounds; otherwise the arpeggio sounds like a strum.
- **Slurs at the ends:** where the arpeggio turns around, two notes on the same string are played with a hammer-on and a pull-off, so you do not have to change direction twice (look at the swept arpeggio exercise).
- **Slowly and with a metronome.** Sweeping at a slow tempo seems easy and falls apart at a fast tempo if the notes are not even.

In jazz it is used sparingly: in passing arpeggios, in the lines of Pat Martino or Frank Gambale and at the ends of phrases.

## Practising with the metronome

The most effective way to gain speed is the most boring one: **slow, clean and going up little by little**.

1. **Find the clean tempo.** The tempo at which you play the exercise three times in a row without a mistake, relaxed. That is the starting point, not the tempo at which it "almost works".
2. **Go up a little.** 4 or 5 BPM every two or four rounds. If a step goes wrong, go back two steps and climb again.
3. **Set the limit** a bit above your best tempo today. You do not have to reach it every day.
4. **Watch the stroke.** Before going up, check against the tab that the pick does what it says: the typical mistake is switching technique without noticing on the string changes.
5. **Vary the subdivision.** The same exercise in eighths, triplets, sixteenths and sextuplets: it changes where the accents fall and forces you to listen to the pulse, not just move the hand.

In the exercise: set the starting tempo with the tempo control, how much to raise it, every how many rounds and up to where, and press "Tempo ramp". The four-beat count-in gives you time to place your hand.

## Relaxation and sound

- **Little pick showing.** Let only a little stick out from your fingers: the less it protrudes, the shorter the travel and the more control.
- **Small motion.** From the wrist (or the forearm), not from the fingers; the pick should not move further from the strings than necessary.
- **No tension.** If the shoulder or forearm tightens as the tempo rises, stop and go down. Tension is the real limit on speed.
- **Even attack.** Every note at the same volume; record yourself and listen.
- **A thick pick** (1.5 mm or more) in jazz: it gives a rounder sound and bends less, which helps with sweeps.

## How to practise it

1. **Chromatic 1-2-3-4** with alternate picking, in sixteenths, from 60 to 100 BPM raising 4 every two rounds. It is the warm-up.
2. **Two-per-string pentatonic.** Alternate: going up every change is inside and going down outside. Reverse the direction (start on the 1st string) to practise the other.
3. **Scale in position** with strict alternate picking: the changes alternate between inside and outside. It is the most useful exercise for jazz.
4. **Three per string** in triplets with alternate and then with economy: compare the feel and the sound.
5. **Swept arpeggio** very slowly, in sextuplets, making sure each note is muted. Speed it up only when it sounds like separate notes and not like a chord.
6. **Apply it.** Take a phrase from a solo (from the Bebop page, for example) and decide the stroke for each note before playing it fast.
