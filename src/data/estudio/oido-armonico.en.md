## Hearing harmony

An ear for intervals recognises two notes. A **harmonic ear** recognises chords: what **quality** they have (major, minor, dominant…) and what **function** they play in the key (I, IV, V…). It is what lets you work out the chords of a tune by ear, follow a form without a chart and know, when you improvise, where the music is.

The trainer has three games, from easiest to hardest:

1. **Quality.** A chord sounds (as a block and arpeggiated) and you say what type it is.
2. **Chain.** The tonic sounds, then some chords from the key; you name the degree of each one.
3. **Progression.** A whole progression from the [Progressions](../../progresiones/) catalogue sounds and you have to recognise it.

## Quality: the colour of the chord

Start with few options and add more once you are almost always right:

- **Major and minor triads.** Major, bright; minor, dark. If in doubt, sing the 3.
- **Diminished and augmented.** Diminished sounds tight (two minor thirds); augmented, open and restless (two major thirds).
- **The four jazz seventh chords:** maj7 (soft, with the 7 a half step below the root), 7 (tense, wants to resolve: it is the tritone between 3 and b7), m7 (relaxed) and m7b5 (an uneasy minor).
- **Then:** °7 (symmetric, dramatic), m6 and mMaj7 (tonic minor chords), 6, 7sus4 (a dominant with no 3, suspended).

A tip for the usual confusions: **maj7 versus 7**, listen to the top note of the arpeggio: a half step below the octave (maj7) or a whole step (7). **m7 versus m7b5**, the 5: stable or sinking.

## Chain: the function of each chord

What matters here is the **relationship with the tonic**, not the chord on its own. The chain always starts on **I**, which sets the key; from there, each chord has a role:

| Function | Degrees | How it sounds |
|---|---|---|
| Tonic | I, IIIm, VIm | At rest; VIm is a sad rest |
| Subdominant | IV, IIm | Moving away from home, without urgency |
| Dominant | V7, VIIm7b5 | Tension that wants to go back to I |

Tricks to get started:

- **Sing the tonic** over each chord: it is part of I, IIm7, IV and VIm, and it clashes with V7 and VIIm7b5, which want to go back.
- **Follow the bass.** The degrees are the root relative to the tonic: if the bass drops a 5th (or rises a 4th), the usual move is ii–V–I.
- **V7 is recognised by its tension**; IIm7, by coming just before V7.
- **With chords from outside the key** (in the settings): VI7 and II7 sound like dominants leading to IIm and V; bVII7 is the *backdoor*; IVm6, the minor subdominant (bolero melancholy).

## Progression: recognising the whole road

In the end you hear whole **blocks**: ii–V–I, turnaround, Andalusian cadence, blues… Choose in the settings which ones are included. Practise first with very different ones (blues versus ii–V–I) and then with similar ones (turnaround I–VI–II–V versus III–VI–II–V).

## How to practise it

- **Little and often.** Five minutes a day are worth more than an hour a week.
- **Sing before you answer.** Sing the root and the tonic: the ear is trained by singing, not by guessing.
- **Fixed tonic first, random later.** With a fixed tonic you learn the relationships; with a random one you check you are not just memorising the notes.
- **Take it to the guitar.** After each answer, play the chain on the guitar: that way the sound and the shape become linked.
- **Work out tunes.** The goal is the repertoire: listen to a tune, find the tonic and sing the bass; that is the same chain exercise with real music.
