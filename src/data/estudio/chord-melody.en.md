## The idea

In **chord melody** the guitar plays the **melody** (in the top voice) and the **harmony** (underneath) at the same time, like a pianist with both hands. It is the way to play a tune on your own, without accompaniment: the style of George Van Eps, Joe Pass, Lenny Breau, Ted Greene or Martin Taylor.

There is one rule: **the melody has to be the highest note**. If the chord has a note above the melody, the melody gets lost. That is why the usual shapes don't work: for each melody note you have to find a shape of the chord that has it on top.

In the example above you choose the chord and the melody note, and you see every shape that has it in the top voice, from the lowest to the highest on the neck. Press one to hear it: the chord sounds and then the melody alone, to check that it is heard on top.

## How a shape is built

- **The melody on the 1st or 2nd string** (sometimes the 3rd). That keeps it in the high register and leaves room underneath.
- **Underneath, the 3 and the 7 of the chord.** They are the notes that say which chord it is; with them and the melody the harmony is already clear.
- **The root in the bass, if it fits.** On the 6th or 5th string. If it doesn't fit (or there is a bass player), it can go: the ear fills it in.
- **Different notes.** With three or four notes, each must add something: no doublings and no repeating the melody.
- **In one hand.** At most a four-fret span. Strings that don't sound are muted with the spare fingers.

## Melody, chord and tensions

The melody note has a role over the chord, and the example shows it:

- **Chord tone** (1, 3, 5, 7): the simplest; any shape of the chord with that note on top.
- **Tension** (9, 11, 13 and the altered ones on the dominant): sounds great in the top voice; it is the typical colour of jazz. Underneath go the 3 and the 7, and the root if it fits.
- **Clashing note** (for example F over Cmaj7: the 11, a half step above the 3): do not harmonise it with that chord. Either play it alone, as a passing note, or change the chord: F over Dm7 (b3) or over Fmaj7 (1) sounds good.

Turn on "Tensions underneath" so the inner voices can carry a 9 or a 13: you get more modern shapes (maj9, 13).

## How to arrange a tune

1. **Raise the melody an octave** if needed, so it falls on the 1st and 2nd strings.
2. **Harmonise the strong beats.** One chord at each chord change and on the long notes; there is no need to harmonise every note.
3. **Between chords, melody only.** Passing notes sound alone, or with the bass.
4. **Change shape, not position.** Choose shapes close to each other on the neck so the hand moves little.
5. **Fill with the bass.** On the long melody notes, move the bass (root, 5, a chromatic line) so the music does not stop.

## How to practise it

1. **One chord, every note.** With Cmaj7, go through the available melody notes (1, 9, 3, #11, 5, 13, 7) and play a shape for each one in the same area of the neck.
2. **The 3 and 7 underneath.** Play with three notes only (melody, 3 and 7) so your ear gets used to not needing the root.
3. **A tune from the Repertoire.** Choose a tune with a clear melody (for example *I'll See You in My Dreams*) and harmonise the first-beat notes of each bar with the chord from the chart.
4. **Slowly and singing.** Play the shape and sing the melody at the same time: if the top note does not match what you sing, the shape is wrong.

## Ideas for variety

- **Drop 2 shapes with the melody on top:** many of the shapes in the example are [drop 2](../drop2/) on strings 4-3-2-1; learn their four inversions and you will have one for each chord tone.
- **Reharmonise the melody:** a long melody note allows several chords (see [Reharmonization](../rearmonizacion/)); try the same note over three different chords in the example.
- **Triads on top:** with the melody on the 1st string, a [triad](../triadas/) on strings 3-2-1 over an open bass is the simplest chord melody.
- **Bass line:** in slow tunes, alternate bass and chord (bass on 1 and 3, chord with melody on 2 and 4).
