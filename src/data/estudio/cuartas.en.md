## The idea

Almost every chord you study is built in **thirds**: 1, 3, 5, 7. A **quartal** chord stacks fourths instead of thirds: D, G, C, F. The result has no 3rd to say whether it is major or minor, which is why it sounds open, modern and ambiguous: it is the sound of 1960s modal jazz.

The reference pianist is **McCoy Tyner**, with John Coltrane's quartet: over a one-chord vamp, the left hand plays fourths and moves them along the scale, in parallel, while the bass holds the pedal note. Herbie Hancock (*Maiden Voyage*) and Chick Corea also used them a lot, and today they are part of the basic language of any accompanist.

In the example above you choose a mode and a string group, and see the quartal voicings of that scale climbing the neck, labelled with their degree over the vamp chord. They sound with the root as a pedal in the bass.

## On the guitar it is easy

The guitar is tuned in fourths (E, A, D, G), except the 2nd string (B), which is a major third above the 3rd. So:

- **On strings 6-5-4-3, a perfect fourth on each string is the same fret.** Perfect-fourth voicings are a one-finger barre.
- **When crossing to the 2nd string, the note goes up a fret.** On strings 4-3-2 or 3-2-1, the shape has the 2nd-string note one fret higher.
- **Augmented fourth.** Stacking fourths within the scale, one in seven is augmented (in D Dorian, from B to F). In the shape, that note is one fret higher. If you don't want it, tick "Perfect fourths only".

## Modal uses

Since they have no 3rd, quartal voicings work over several chords. What decides which chord sounds is **the bass**:

| Voicing | Over D (Dm7) | Over G (G7sus4) | Over C (Cmaj7) |
|---|---|---|---|
| E A D | 9 5 1 | 13 9 5 | 3 13 9 |
| A D G | 5 1 11 | 9 5 1 | 13 9 5 |
| D G C | 1 11 b7 | 5 1 11 | 9 5 1 |

The three modes in the example are where they are used most:

- **Dorian (m7).** The mode of *So What* and *Impressions*. Over a D bass, the fourths of the scale sound like Dm11 in almost every position.
- **Mixolydian sus (7sus4).** The sus chord is the quartal chord par excellence: it has the 4 and no 3, just like fourths. It is the sound of *Maiden Voyage*.
- **Lydian (maj7#11).** With the #11, fourths sound very open and bright.

## The So What chord

On *So What* (Miles Davis, 1959), Bill Evans plays a voicing that has become famous: **three perfect fourths and a major third on top**. Over Dm: E A D G B (9 5 1 11 13). The answer to the theme ("so what") is two of these chords, the second a whole step lower.

On the guitar, on strings 6-5-4-3-2, that structure is **the same shape on every fret**, because the 2nd string is already a major third up: a five-string barre. Choose "So What" in the example to see it on the scale degrees where it fits.

## How to comp with fourths

- **Parallelism (planing).** Move the same voicing along the scale, note by note, without changing the chord in the bass. It is the ladder in the example.
- **Neighbours.** Alternate one voicing and the next one (the "Neighbours" pattern). Very common as a rhythmic figure over a vamp.
- **Pedal.** Let the bass (or your open 6th or 5th string) hold the root and move the fourths on top.
- **Little register.** With three notes on the high strings, fourths leave room for the soloist and sound less dense.

## How to practise it

1. **One ladder, one string group.** Play the example's ladder up and down, with the pedal in the background. Notice which shape changes when the augmented fourth appears.
2. **The four groups.** Do the same on 6-5-4, 5-4-3, 4-3-2 and 3-2-1. The shapes change when crossing the 2nd string; the note names do not.
3. **Neighbours with rhythm.** Choose a voicing, the "Neighbours" pattern, and comp with the rhythms from [Rhythm](../ritmo/) (Charleston, anticipations).
4. **In a tune.** Over the Fm7 bars of *Cantaloupe Island*, or over a Dm7 vamp, comp only with fourths from the Dorian scale.
5. **The So What chord.** Learn the five-string shape and play it over Dm7 on E and on D (the "amen").

## Ideas for variety

- **Mix fourths and triads.** Alternate quartal voicings with [triads](../triadas/) from the same scale: open and closed.
- **Improvise with fourths.** Play lines jumping fourth by fourth along the scale (in [Pentatonics in jazz](../pentatonicas/), the "skipping a note" figure gives fourths).
- **Chromatic fourths.** Shift a voicing up a half step and back (see [Outside and in](../outside/)): McCoy Tyner did it constantly.
- **Another bass.** With the same ladder, change the pedal note: over another root, the same voicings are another chord (the table above).
