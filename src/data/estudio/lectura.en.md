## The idea

Sight-reading means playing a piece of music you have not seen before, in time and without stopping. On the guitar it is harder than on other instruments for one reason: **each note is in several places on the neck**. The pianist sees a note and has one key; the guitarist has up to five, and has to choose.

That is why the first step is neither rhythm nor speed, but something more basic: **seeing a note on the staff and knowing at once where it is in the area of the neck where your hand is**.

In the exercise above a note appears on the staff and you have to find it on the neck by pressing its fret. Any string will do, but **in its octave**: if you play the same note an octave higher or lower, it tells you. When you get it (or with "I don't know") every place where the note is appears. You can limit the area to one position or one string and choose whether accidentals appear.

## The guitar sounds an octave lower

Guitar music is written in **treble clef**, but **an octave above how it sounds**. That way most notes fall within the staff instead of piling up ledger lines below it. Sometimes this is shown with a small 8 under the clef; often it is not shown and is taken for granted.

Some reference points, as they are written:

| On the neck | On the staff |
|---|---|
| Open 6th string (E) | E with three ledger lines below |
| Open 5th string (A) | A with two ledger lines below |
| Open 4th string (D) | D, just below the staff |
| Open 3rd string (G) | G, on the 2nd line |
| Open 2nd string (B) | B, on the 3rd line (the middle one) |
| Open 1st string (E) | E, in the 4th space |
| 1st string, 5th fret (A) | A with one ledger line above |
| 1st string, 12th fret (E) | E on the third ledger line above |

A quick rule: **the open-string notes** are the first ones to recognise without thinking. From them, the rest are found by counting frets.

## Reading by positions

Reading on the guitar is organised by **positions** (see [Fingering and positions](../digitacion/)). In a position, with one finger per fret, each written note has **a single place**: the ambiguity disappears. That is why reading methods (Leavitt's, for example) teach position by position.

- **Start with position 5** (or 2): no open strings and almost every note inside the staff.
- **When one position comes easily, move to the next.** There is no need to go in order; the most useful are 2, 5, 7 and 9 or 10.
- **One string** is a different exercise: covering the neck horizontally. It helps fix where each note is along the string and for melodies that climb a lot.

In the exercise, choose "Position" and the starting fret; the notes that appear are all within that area (although any place where the note sounds is accepted).

## Notes, not numbers

When reading, try to think of the **note** and not the fret: "this is an F#, in this position it is on the 4th string, 4th fret". In time the intermediate step disappears and the eye goes straight to the finger.

Some hints that help:

- **Lines and spaces.** The notes on the lines (E, G, B, D, F) and in the spaces (F, A, C, E) form two fixed groups. Knowing the line notes by heart speeds things up a lot.
- **By intervals.** In a melody, after the first note the next one is usually close: if it moves up one space, it is a step; if it jumps from line to line, it is a 3rd. Reading the shape of the line is faster than naming every note.
- **The key signature.** The accidentals in the key signature apply to the whole piece: before starting, look at which they are and where they fall in your position.

## Rhythm and continuity

This exercise trains the step from the note to the neck. Real reading adds rhythm, and there the golden rule is **don't stop**:

1. **Choose a slow tempo** at which you can play without stopping. Better a wrong note in time than the right one late.
2. **Look ahead.** While you play one note, your eye is already on the next (or on the next bar).
3. **Read the rhythm before the notes.** If in doubt, play the rhythm on a single note first (see [Rhythm](../ritmo/)).
4. **Learn the typical jazz figures:** off-beat eighths, anticipations, triplets. Read many times, they are recognised as a shape, not note by note.

## Reading in jazz

In a jazz group you mostly read in two ways:

- **Melodies** of tunes (lead sheets), usually with chord symbols above. Reading them well lets you play a new tune at a session or a rehearsal.
- **Chord charts** (changes): what a comping guitarist reads most. Here there are no notes, only chords, and what you need is to find voicings quickly (see the Chords pages and [Shells](../shells/)).

A good exercise that joins both: read the melody of a standard from the Repertoire in two or three different positions and choose the one that fits the chords best.

## How to practise it

1. **Ten minutes a day.** Reading improves with frequency, not with long sessions.
2. **One position a week.** In the exercise, a fixed position and naturals only until your streak passes 20 and your average time drops below 2 seconds. Then with sharps and flats.
3. **Each string separately.** "One string", from the 6th to the 1st: that is where the notes on the high frets are missed most.
4. **The whole neck.** "Frets 0–12": find the note anywhere, but in its octave. Notice the places that appear when you answer.
5. **Real music.** Read simple melodies (folk songs, reading method books, the melodies in the Repertoire) in a fixed position without stopping, with a metronome.
6. **Read something new every day.** Rereading the same thing trains memory, not reading.
