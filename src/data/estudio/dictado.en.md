## Call and response

In jazz you learn by playing with others: someone plays a phrase and you answer it. **Melodic dictation** turns that into an exercise: a one-bar phrase sounds (the **call**) and in the next bar you play it back the same (the **response**), without stopping the pulse. Next time round a different phrase sounds, and so on without end.

It is the most direct way to join ear and hand: there is no time to think of note names, only to hear, remember and find the sound on the neck. It is what [Transcription](../transcripcion/) asks of you, but small and in time.

## How it works

1. Press **▶ Start**. A count-in bar sounds (four clicks) and the call begins.
2. **👂 Listen**: the phrase sounds with the backing.
3. **🎸 Your turn**: the backing carries on alone and you play the phrase. The lit beat tells you where you are.
4. The next round brings a new phrase, over the next bar of the context.

With **Each phrase: twice or three times**, the same phrase repeats before changing: the first time you look for it and the second you confirm it. **↺ Hear the phrase** plays it once with no response bar, so you can hear it calmly; **↻ Another phrase** changes it.

Every phrase fits in the chosen **position** (index finger on that fret, from one fret below to four above), so you never have to jump around the neck to play them.

## Help on the neck

Four levels, from most help to none:

- **On show:** the phrase is on the neck and in the bar all the time, and the sounding note is marked. Useful to start and for new language: you hear and see at once.
- **On your turn:** you listen without looking and, when your bar comes, the notes appear and which one to play at each moment. First you try to get it by ear; the help arrives just when you need it.
- **Language notes:** the phrase is hidden, but the neck shows, in the position, every note it can come from: those of the chosen language over the sounding chord (the scale, the arpeggio, the bebop scale, the 3 and the 7…). The main ones, the chord tones, are filled in. Instead of searching the whole neck you choose among a few notes: the step before playing purely by ear.
- **By ear:** nothing is shown. If you get stuck, **👁 Show the phrase** reveals it until it changes.

The **label** of each note on the neck can be the **degree** over the chord (the most instructive), the note **name** or the **order** in which it sounds.

## Harmonic contexts

The same phrase sounds different over another chord, and the ear has to learn to hear it against the harmony:

- **Vamps**: a single chord. The easiest way to start: the ear only follows the melody. There is one for each colour: major (Imaj7, I6), minor (Im7 Dorian, Im6, ImMaj7), dominant (I7, I7sus4, I7alt, I7#5 augmented), half-diminished (Im7b5) and diminished (I°7).
- **Cadences**: the **ii–V in one bar** (the phrase crosses the chord change on beat 3) and every one in the [Progressions](../../progresiones/) catalogue, bar by bar (including the ones you have edited): major and minor ii–V–I, turnarounds, blues, rhythm changes…

## Aspects of the language

Each aspect generates phrases with one specific device, so you can work on them separately:

| Aspect | What sounds | Where to study it |
|---|---|---|
| Arpeggios | Only chord tones | [Arpeggios](../arpegios/) |
| Scale | Consecutive degrees of the chord's scale, with chord tones on the beats | [Scales on the neck](../escalas/) |
| Bebop | Eighth notes along the bebop scale, almost always descending | [Bebop language](../bebop/) |
| Approaches | Chord tones on the beats, preceded by a half step or the scale note above | [Approaches](../aproximaciones/) |
| Guide tones | The 3 and the 7, with the smallest movement when the chord changes | [Guide tones](../notas-guia/) |
| Tensions | 9, 11 and 13 on the strong beats, resolving by step | [Tensions](../tensiones/) |
| Blues | The blues scale of the key over every chord | [Blues](../blues/) |

The **difficulty** changes the number of notes, the rhythm and the range: on easy, three or four notes in quarter notes and little more than an octave; on hard, up to eight eighth notes with anticipations, across the whole range of the position and with bigger leaps.

## How to practise it

- **Sing before you play.** If you can sing the response, you have it; the neck is just finding where it is. At first, sing in your bar and play on the repeat (Each phrase: twice).
- **Slow tempo.** Start at 60–70 and go up when you get almost all of them. What matters is not stopping: if you miss a note, keep the rhythm going.
- **The contour first.** Does it go up or down? Does it leap or move by step? With the contour and the first note, the rest follows.
- **The first note.** Hear where it falls relative to the chord: root, 3, 5, 7? With the label set to degrees you will check it at once.
- **One aspect at a time.** A week of arpeggios over the ii–V–I, another of approaches… When an aspect is easy on "On your turn", move it to "By ear".
- **Change key and position.** The ear should not depend on one key, nor the hand on one area of the neck.

## Common mistakes

- **Stopping the pulse to think.** The response is in time, as in a band. Better a wrong note in time than the right one late.
- **Always looking.** "On show" is for learning the language; the ear exercise begins when you stop looking.
- **Paying attention only to the notes.** Rhythm is dictated too: where the phrase starts (on 1 or off the beat) and how long the last note lasts.
- **Moving up in difficulty too fast.** Three notes heard properly teach more than eight guessed.
