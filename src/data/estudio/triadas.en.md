## Why triads

Three notes: **root, 3 and 5**. They are the smallest chord that sounds complete and the building block for everything else: seventh chords are a triad plus one note, and many jazz voicings are a triad on top of another note. Mick Goodrick insists on them because, with only three notes, you can clearly see how each voice moves and you learn the whole neck in a few shapes.

Four types (plus sus4, which has no 3):

| Type | Degrees | In C |
|---|---|---|
| Major | 1 3 5 | C E G |
| Minor | 1 b3 5 | C Eb G |
| Diminished | 1 b3 b5 | C Eb Gb |
| Augmented | 1 3 #5 | C E G# |
| Sus4 | 1 4 5 | C F G |

## Close: four string groups

A **close triad** has its three notes within one octave and is played on three adjacent strings. There are four groups (6-5-4, 5-4-3, 4-3-2 and 3-2-1) and in each one, three **inversions** depending on the bass note: root position (1), first (3) and second (5). Twelve shapes per triad type.

C major on the top strings (frets from the 6th to the 1st string; x = not played):

| Strings | Root position (C E G) | 1st inversion (E G C) | 2nd inversion (G C E) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3-2-1 | `x x x 5 5 3` | `x x x 9 8 8` | `x x x 12 13 12` |
| 4-3-2 | `x x 10 9 8 x` | `x x 14 12 13 x` | `x x 5 5 5 x` |

- **Vertically**, without moving the hand, in a five-fret area there is one inversion on each string group.
- **Horizontally**, on one string group, the three inversions climb the neck and start again at the octave. The top note runs through the triad: 5, 1, 3, 5…
- Major shapes become minor by lowering the 3 a half step (one finger), augmented by raising the 5 and diminished by lowering the 3 and the 5.

## Open

In an **open triad** the middle voice of the close triad goes up (or down) an octave: the notes are further apart and the sound is wider, like a string section. They are played on three strings with one skipped: between the bass and the other two (6-4-3, 5-3-2 and 4-2-1) or between the top two (6-5-3, 5-4-2 and 4-3-1).

| | From low to high |
|---|---|
| Close | C · **E** · G |
| Open | C · G · **E** (the E goes up an octave) |

C major open:

| Strings | Root position (C G E) | 1st inversion (E C G) | 2nd inversion (G E C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4-2-1 | `x x 10 x 8 12` | `x x 2 x 1 3` | `x x 5 x 5 8` |
| 6-4-3 | `8 x 5 9 x x` | `12 x 10 12 x x` | `3 x 2 5 x x` |
| 4-3-1 | `x x 10 12 x 12` | `x x 2 5 x 3` | `x x 5 9 x 8` |
| 6-5-3 | `8 10 x 9 x x` | `12 15 x 12 x x` | `3 7 x 5 x x` |

It is the same idea as the four-note [drop 2](../drop2/) and [drop 3](../drop3/): spreading the voices by moving one of them an octave.

## Triads over other chords

A triad played over another bass gives a bigger chord. It is the simplest way to get **tensions**: you play a familiar shape and the result sounds like modern jazz. Over C:

| Over | Triad | Notes | Chord degrees |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cmaj7 | Em | E G B | 3 5 7 |
| Cmaj7 | G | G B D | 5 7 9 |
| Cm7 | Eb | Eb G Bb | b3 5 b7 |
| Cm7 | Gm | G Bb D | 5 b7 9 |
| C7 | Gm | G Bb D | 5 b7 9 |
| C7 | D | D F# A | 9 #11 13 |
| C7 | Ab | Ab C Eb | b13 1 #9 (altered) |
| C7 | Gb | Gb Bb Db | b5 b7 b9 (tritone substitute) |

The ones at the top are called *upper structures*. In the example above, the progression is played with each chord's triad; to try these, play the triad from the table instead of the one shown.

## How to practise them

1. **One triad, one string group.** C major on 3-2-1: the three inversions going up and down, saying which note is on top. Then minor, diminished and augmented.
2. **The four groups vertically.** In one area of the neck (for example frets 5-8), find the triad on the four string groups without moving the hand.
3. **One note per string.** Arpeggiate the triad one note per string, from the 6th to the 1st and back, changing inversion so you never skip a string (the [routine](../rutina/) exercise, block A2).
4. **The harmonised scale.** On one string group, play the triads of the major scale in order: C Dm Em F G Am B° C, in the inversion closest to the previous one. Then go through them by [Goodrick cycles](../ciclos/).
5. **Progressions.** The classical progressions in the Progressions section (cadences, Pachelbel, Andalusian, Folia) are written in triads: play them on a single string group with the example above and follow the top voice.
6. **Upper structures.** In a ii–V–I, play F (over Dm7), Dm (over G7: 5 b7 9) and Em or G (over Cmaj7) with a bass or the metronome. Listen to how the tensions sound.
7. **In the repertoire.** Comp a standard using only triads on strings 3-2-1: one triad per chord, the closest to the previous one.

## Ideas for variety

- **Triads on one string.** Play the triad on a single string (as in Goodrick's *unitar*): 1, 3 and 5 along the neck.
- **Two triads.** Alternate two nearby triads (C and Dm, or G and Am) in a fixed rhythm: it is the sound of many soul and funk vamps.
- **Triad + open bass.** In A, E or D, play the triads of the scale on the top strings with the low string open: a pedal that sounds modal.
