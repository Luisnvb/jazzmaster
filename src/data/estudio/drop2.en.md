## What it is

A four-note chord in **close position** (all within one octave: C E G B) is almost unplayable on the guitar: it forces the hand to stretch across adjacent strings. **Drop 2** fixes that: it takes the **second voice from the top** and drops it an octave.

| | From low to high |
|---|---|
| Close | C · E · **G** · B |
| Drop 2 | **G** · C · E · B |

The chord now spans a little more than an octave and each note falls on a different one of **four adjacent strings**. It sounds more open than close position, with no gaps, and it is the most used four-note voicing in jazz guitar.

- **Drop 3** is the same with the third voice from the top: the bass is separated and it is played on string groups with one skipped (6-4-3-2, 5-3-2-1). It is in [Drop 3](../drop3/).
- The [Goodrick cycles](../ciclos/) have a *Drop 2* button to hear the same voice leading in this voicing.

## Inversions and string groups

Each chord has **four inversions**, depending on the note in the bass, and can be played on **three string groups**: 4-3-2-1, 5-4-3-2 and 6-5-4-3. That makes 12 shapes per chord type.

Cmaj7 on strings 4-3-2-1 (frets from the 4th to the 1st string):

| Inversion | Bass | Frets | Notes | Top |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1st inversion | 3 | `2 4 1 3` | E B C G | 5 |
| 2nd inversion | 5 | `5 5 5 7` | G C E B | 7 |
| 3rd inversion | 7 | `9 9 8 8` | B E G C | 1 |
| Root position | 1 | `10 12 12 12` | C G B E | 3 |

Cmaj7 on strings 5-4-3-2 (frets from the 5th to the 2nd string):

| Inversion | Bass | Frets | Notes | Top |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Root position | 1 | `3 5 4 5` | C G B E | 3 |
| 1st inversion | 3 | `7 9 5 8` | E B C G | 5 |
| 2nd inversion | 5 | `10 10 9 12` | G C E B | 7 |
| 3rd inversion | 7 | `14 14 12 13` | B E G C | 1 |

Two things to notice:

- **Going up the neck, the top note runs through the chord**: 3, 5, 7, 1… Each inversion puts another chord tone in the melody.
- **The shapes repeat**: the Cmaj7 one works for any maj7; for C7, m7 or m7b5 only one or two fingers change (the 7 drops a half step, the 3 drops a half step…). Learn one quality completely and the others come by moving one note.

## How they connect

In a progression there is no need to jump around the neck: at each change you choose the **closest** inversion, and the voices move in small steps, as with [guide tones](../notas-guia/). A ii–V–I in C on strings 4-3-2-1:

| | Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frets (4th→1st) | `7 7 6 8` | `5 7 6 7` | `5 5 5 7` |
| Notes | A D F C | G D F B | G C E B |

The top voice goes C → B → B (the 7 of Dm7 drops to the 3 of G7, which stays as the 7 of Cmaj7) and the hand stays between frets 5 and 8. The example above does this with any progression and on any string group.

## How to practise them

1. **One quality, one string group.** Start with maj7 on 4-3-2-1: the four inversions from low to high and back, saying which note is in the bass and which on top.
2. **The five qualities.** On the same four shapes, change the quality: maj7 → 7 → m7 → m7b5 → °7. Notice which finger moves each time.
3. **The other string groups.** Repeat on 5-4-3-2 (the most used for comping) and on 6-5-4-3 (lower, for dark sounds or when there is no bass).
4. **ii–V–I in one area.** Play the ii–V–I in all twelve keys without leaving five frets, choosing the closest inversion at each change.
5. **Melody on top.** Choose which note you want in the top voice (the 3 of the first chord, for example) and find the inversion that has it. That is how you harmonise a melody: the basis of chord melody and of guitar soli.
6. **Comping rhythm.** Charleston, anticipations on the "&" of 4, and short hits. With drop 2 the comping already sounds full; leave space.
7. **In the repertoire.** Play a whole standard with drop 2 on 5-4-3-2 and then on 4-3-2-1. Compare how the melody of the top voice changes.

## Ideas for variety

- **Tensions instead of chord tones.** Replace the root with the 9 (on maj7, m7 and 7) or the 5 with the 13 (on 7): same fingers, more modern sound.
- **Inversions along the scale.** Keep the chord and move the top voice along the scale, changing inversion on each note: a chord that "sings".
- **Drop 2 by cycles.** Play Goodrick's cycles in drop 2 on one string group, without leaving one area.
