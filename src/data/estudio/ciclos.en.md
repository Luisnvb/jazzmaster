## What a cycle is

Mick Goodrick organises diatonic progressions according to **how far the root rises** from one chord to the next. In his *Almanac of Guitar Voice-Leading* (*Mr. Goodchord*) he uses this idea to go through every chord in the key and studies how the voices move in each case.

A cycle starts on I, passes through the seven degrees of the scale and returns to I. All the chords are diatonic, so the quality (maj7, m7, 7, m7b5) is decided by the degree, not the cycle.

| Cycle | The root… | In C (seventh chords) |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | rises a second (by step) | Cmaj7 Dm7 Em7 Fmaj7 G7 Am7 Bm7b5 Cmaj7 |
| 3 | rises a third | Cmaj7 Em7 G7 Bm7b5 Dm7 Fmaj7 Am7 Cmaj7 |
| 4 | rises a fourth (= falls a fifth) | Cmaj7 Fmaj7 Bm7b5 Em7 Am7 Dm7 G7 Cmaj7 |
| 5 | rises a fifth (= falls a fourth) | Cmaj7 G7 Dm7 Am7 Em7 Bm7b5 Fmaj7 Cmaj7 |
| 6 | rises a sixth (= falls a third) | Cmaj7 Am7 Fmaj7 Dm7 Bm7b5 G7 Em7 Cmaj7 |
| 7 | rises a seventh (= falls by step) | Cmaj7 Bm7b5 Am7 G7 Fmaj7 Em7 Dm7 Cmaj7 |

The cycles come **in pairs**: 2 and 7, 3 and 6, 4 and 5 are the same journey read backwards. So mastering three is enough to have all six.

## Why it matters

- **Cycle 4 is functional harmony.** ii–V–I, iii–vi–ii–V, chains of dominants and most standards move by fourths. Practising the whole of cycle 4 is practising the most common movement in jazz on every degree.
- **The other cycles widen the ear.** Cycle 2 appears in bass lines and stepwise passages, 3 and 6 in substitutions (iii or vi for I) and in modal progressions, 5 in rock and pop.
- **Each cycle has its own voice leading.** What moves and what stays still depends on the cycle, not the key. Once you learn how the voices behave in a cycle, you will recognise it in any tune.

## Common tones in each cycle

If each voice goes to the nearest note of the next chord, the number of notes that stay still is always the same in each cycle. Check it in the example above: the common tones are marked in the accent colour.

| Cycle | Triads | Seventh chords | How the others move |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 and 7 | 0 | 1 | all by step, in the same direction |
| 3 and 6 | 2 | 3 | only one voice moves, by step |
| 4 and 5 | 1 | 2 | by step (in cycle 4, the 7 falls to the 3 of the next chord) |

Cycle 4 with seventh chords is the **guide-tone** rule: in Dm7 → G7 the F (3 of Dm7) stays as the 7 of G7, and the C (7 of Dm7) falls a half step to B (3 of G7).

## How to practise them

1. **Listen first.** Loop the cycle with a sustained sound (organ, strings) and follow one of the lines with your voice: the top one, then an inner one. Sing it without the audio.
2. **Triads on one string group.** Choose a group of three adjacent strings (for example 4-3-2) and play the cycle moving each voice to the nearest note. Do not change string group. When you finish, repeat starting from another inversion of I: you get three different journeys per group.
3. **Seventh chords in drop 2.** Drop 2 is the most comfortable four-note voicing on the guitar: on strings 4-3-2-1 and 5-4-3-2. Use the example's *Drop 2* button to see which notes sound and find them on the neck. The shapes in **Chords** help you find them.
4. **Pay attention to the top voice.** The highest note of each chord forms a melody. Each cycle, each starting inversion and each string group gives a different melody: a source of lines for comping.
5. **Slowly and in free time, then with the metronome.** First get every change without looking; then at two beats per chord.
6. **In every key.** Cycle 4 through all twelve keys is a classic warm-up.
7. **Take it to the repertoire.** Find the passages in your tunes that move by fourths (almost every ii–V) or by thirds and play them with the same voice leading.

## Ideas for variety

- **One cycle, one string.** Play only the top voice of the cycle on a single string: the melody is seen as distances.
- **Two voices.** Play only the guide tones (3 and 7) in cycle 4: the skeleton of the whole progression with two fingers.
- **Cycles with another scale.** The same journeys with the chords of harmonic minor or melodic minor completely change the colour.
- **Improvising over the cycle.** On a loop, improvise using only the common tones at each change, then only the ones that move.
