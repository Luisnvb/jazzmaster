## Scales for improvising

The **Scales** section is a dictionary: which scales fit each chord type. This page is for **practising** them on the neck: where each note is, which notes belong to the chord and how to move through them so they don't sound like an exercise.

A scale is **the chord plus the passing notes between its notes**. On the map above the chord tones are filled in and the rest are hollow: first find the chord inside the scale and then the notes around it.

## Three ways through it

As with [arpeggios](../arpegios/), Mick Goodrick suggests three directions:

- **On one string.** You see the whole and half steps of the scale as real distances. It is the best way to understand why Dorian and Aeolian are so alike (only the 6 changes).
- **On two strings.** The scale climbs diagonally, three or four notes per string. It connects positions without jumps.
- **In position.** Five frets, every string, without moving the hand. The fastest fingering, and the one you have to watch most so you don't always play the same patterns.

There are many fingerings (five-fret positions, three notes per string…). Rather than choosing one, the idea is to know, anywhere, **which degree you are playing**.

## Hearing it over its chord

A scale is not heard on its own: it is heard over a chord. Turn on *Pedal note* in the example (the root sounding underneath, best with a sustained sound such as organ or strings) and play the scale on top:

- **The chord tones** sound stable: they are where you rest.
- **The others** create tension: some are tensions that sound good held (9, 11 in minor, 13) and others want to resolve (the 4 over a major chord).
- Compare scales on the **same root**: C Ionian, C Lydian, C Mixolydian, C Dorian… In the same position, notice which notes change. That change is the whole colour of the scale. More in [Modes](../modos/).

## How to practise them

1. **Name the degrees.** Go up and down the scale in one position saying the degrees (1, 2, b3, 4…), not the note names.
2. **Chord tones first.** Within the scale, play only the chord tones (the arpeggio); then only the others; then all of them.
3. **One string, two strings, position.** The same scale in the three ways, in this order.
4. **Sequences.** In thirds, groups of 3 and groups of 4 (in the example, *Route*). There are more in the **Patterns** section.
5. **With a pedal.** Over the sustained root, improvise with the scale, finding which notes you want to dwell on and which only to pass through.
6. **Every position.** The same scale in all twelve positions on the neck, one a day.
7. **In a progression.** In a ii–V–I, change scale with each chord (Dorian, Mixolydian, Ionian) **without changing position**, landing on a chord tone on the strong beat of each change (see [Guide tones](../notas-guia/) and [Chromatic approaches](../aproximaciones/)).

## Ideas for variety

- **The scale in intervals.** Play the scale skipping one note (thirds), two (fourths), three (fifths): each interval gives a different sound.
- **Bebop scale.** Add the major 7 to Mixolydian as a passing note: in eighths, the chord tones fall on the strong beats by themselves.
- **Rhythm before notes.** Choose a one-bar rhythm and play the scale only with that rhythm, changing note on each hit.
- **One scale, every key.** Dorian around the cycle of fourths (C, F, Bb, Eb…) in the same area of the neck.
