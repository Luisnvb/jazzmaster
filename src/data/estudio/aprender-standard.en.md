## The idea

Learning a standard is not memorising a chord chart: it is being able to **sing the melody, know where you are in the form, hear how the harmony moves, comp it and say something of your own over it**. All of that comes from five steps, and the order matters:

1. **Melody**
2. **Form**
3. **Guide tones**
4. **Shells**
5. **Improvisation**

Each step rests on the previous one. The melody gives you the map of the form; the form organises the harmony; the guide tones are the harmony reduced to two lines; the shells are those two lines with a bass; and the improvisation comes out of everything before. Skipping steps (going straight to "which scale goes over each chord") is the fastest way to play a tune for years without really knowing it.

It is the [Practice routine](../rutina/) applied to a tune: small material, sound before shape and always naming the note and its function. The examples on this page use [Days of Wine and Roses](../../repertorio/days-of-wine-and-roses/), from the Repertoire, but it works for any tune.

## Before you start: listen

- **Listen to several versions,** ideally with the melody sung (the lyrics help you phrase) and by different instrumentalists. A vocal version, a guitar version and one on another instrument is a good minimum.
- **Listen without the guitar,** at any time of day, until you can hum the whole melody. This step cannot be skipped or rushed: it is half the work.
- **Check the details:** key, time signature, usual tempo, style (ballad, swing, bossa) and who wrote it. In the Repertoire it is on the tune's page.

## 1. The melody

The melody is the tune. Everything else (chords, solos) revolves around it.

- **Sing it** before playing it. If a fragment will not come out sung, listen to it again.
- **Learn it by ear** if you can (see [Transcription](../transcripcion/)); if not, from sheet music, but always check with the recording how it is phrased. What is written is an approximation.
- **Play it in a single position** first, then in another: an octave apart, on one string, higher up. Each place on the neck gives it a different character.
- **Name the function of the long notes:** is it the 3 of the chord, the 7, a tension (9, #11, 13)? In Days of Wine and Roses, the melody starts on the 7 of Fmaj7 (E) and moves to the #11 of Eb7 (A): right there you hear the colour of the tune.
- **Phrase like a singer:** breathe between phrases, stretch the arrival notes, vary the rhythm a little. A melody "played as written" sounds stiff.

**You know it when** you can play it without looking, in two positions, and sing it while someone else plays the chords.

## 2. The form

With the melody in your head, the form can be heard on its own. Now it has to become conscious.

- **Write the map:** how many bars, which sections (AABA, ABAC, blues…) and where each one starts (see [Standard forms](../formas/), which has Days of Wine and Roses section by section).
- **Look for what repeats.** In an ABAC, the second A is the same as the first: that is 24 bars to learn, not 32. The differences between similar sections (the endings, the end of C) are exactly what gets forgotten most.
- **Mark the arrival points:** where it resolves to the tonic, where it is left open on the dominant, where the turnaround is.
- **Count while you sing the melody** over the audio in Forms or in the Repertoire, with "⟲ Choruses in a row".

**You know it when** you can say, with the recording playing, which bar and section you are in at any moment.

## 3. The guide tones

Before full chords, the harmony reduced to the essentials: the **3** and the **7** of each chord (see [Guide tones](../notas-guia/)).

- **One line:** play one guide tone per chord (the 3 or the 7), always looking for the one closest to the previous note. You get a line that moves by a half or whole step, or stays still.
- **Both lines:** one starting on the 3 and the other on the 7, at the same time. That is how the tune sounds with no bass and nothing else.
- **Sing one line while you play the other.** It is the best harmonic ear exercise there is: afterwards, in the solo, you will hear the changes.
- **With the melody:** play a guide tone on the first beat of each chord and sing the melody over it.

In Days of Wine and Roses, the two lines hardly move: from **Fmaj7** (A and E) to **Eb7** (G and Db) both go down; to **Am7** (G and C), G stays and Db goes down a half step; to **D7** (F# and C), C stays and G goes down a half step; to **Gm7** (Bb and F), both go down. A common note or a small step at each change: that is how the harmony sounds "from the inside".

**You know it when** you can play both guide-tone lines for the whole tune without looking at the chart.

## 4. The shells

Shells (root, 3 and 7; see [Shell voicings](../shells/)) are the guide tones with the bass underneath: the minimal, complete accompaniment.

- **Pick a spot and stay there:** shells with the root on the 6th and 5th strings let you play almost any tune between frets 1 and 8, changing as little as possible from one chord to the next.
- **Chain them with the guide tones from before:** if the 7 goes down to the 3, so does the shell. That way they sound connected rather than jumping.
- **With rhythm:** first whole notes, then quarter notes (Freddie Green style), then the Charleston and other rhythms from [Comping](../comping/).
- **Sing the melody while you comp.** If you get lost, the form is not learned yet: go back to step 2.
- **Later on:** tensions on top of the shell, [Drop 2](../drop2/) and [Chord melody](../chord-melody/) to play melody and chords at once.

**You know it when** you can comp the whole tune in time, with the metronome on 2 and 4, singing the melody.

## 5. Improvisation

Only now. With the melody, the form and the harmony in your ear, improvising is choosing, not searching.

- **Start with the melody:** play it changing the rhythm, adding and removing notes, ornamenting it. It is the most musical kind of improvisation and the one the first soloists used.
- **Guide tones with rhythm:** improvise using only the 3s and 7s of each chord (then a passing note between them). The changes will be heard on their own.
- **Arpeggios and approaches:** the notes of each chord, reached by a half step (see [Chromatic approaches](../aproximaciones/)).
- **Motifs:** a short idea that repeats and transforms through the form (see [Motifs and development](../motivos/)).
- **Scales last:** they help join the important notes, not replace them. On each tune's page in the Repertoire, the improvisation section suggests scales, guide tones and strategies section by section.
- **Record yourself** for a couple of choruses and listen: can you hear the form? Can you hear the changes? Are there phrases with a beginning and an end?

**You know it when** you can improvise two choruses without getting lost and someone who knows the tune would recognise where you are without hearing the chords.

## A plan over several days

There is no need to do it all at once. One way to spread it out, with 20–30 minutes a day:

- **Days 1–2:** listen, sing and learn the melody. Write out the form.
- **Days 3–4:** melody in two positions. Guide tones (one line, then two).
- **Days 5–6:** shells with rhythm, singing the melody. Go over the melody at the start of each session.
- **Day 7 onwards:** improvise, starting by varying the melody. Keep playing the melody and the shells every day.

After that, the tune goes into rotation: play it once a week, each time in a different key or with a different device (block B of the [Routine](../rutina/) is exactly that: the tune in a single position).

## Common mistakes

- **Learning the chords and not the melody.** Without the melody there is no form or phrasing; the solo becomes a string of scales.
- **Always reading from the page.** What you read you do not learn. From the second day, try to play it from memory and look only when you get lost.
- **Learning it in only one key.** Moving the tune to another key (the key selector in Forms, or transposing in the Repertoire) forces you to think in degrees and fixes it in your head.
- **Wanting to play everything fast.** A slow tempo played well teaches more than a fast one stumbling along.
- **Jumping to improvisation.** It is the most fun part and that is why it tempts you; without the previous steps you learn to play *over* the tune, not *the* tune.

## A checklist

To know whether a tune is really learned:

- [ ] I can sing the whole melody without the recording.
- [ ] I can play the melody from memory in two positions.
- [ ] I know the form: sections, bars and where it resolves.
- [ ] I can play both guide-tone lines from memory.
- [ ] I can comp with shells in time, singing the melody.
- [ ] I can improvise two choruses without getting lost.
- [ ] I can play it in another key.
