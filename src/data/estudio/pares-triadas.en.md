## What a triad pair is

Two triads **with no notes in common**. Together they add up to six different notes: a hexatonic scale. Instead of thinking of the whole scale, you improvise by jumping from one triad to the other, and the line comes out with wide intervals (thirds, fourths) and a modern sound that doesn't sound like a scale run up and down.

The idea comes from the saxophone (Coltrane, Michael Brecker, Jerry Bergonzi; Gary Campbell organised it in *Triad Pairs for Jazz*) and it fits the guitar very well: you already know the [triads](../triadas/) all over the neck and each one fits in a small hand position.

The most used are **two major triads a whole step apart** (bVI and bVII, IV and V…), but minor, augmented and diminished ones work too. What matters is how their six notes sound **over the chord**:

| Chord | Pair (in C) | Degrees over the chord | Scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cmaj7#11 | D · Em | 9 #11 13 · 3 5 7 | Lydian |
| Cmaj7 | G · Am | 5 7 9 · 13 1 3 | Ionian without the 4 |
| Cm7 | Eb · F | b3 5 b7 · 11 13 1 | Dorian without the 9 |
| C7 | Gm · Am | 5 b7 9 · 13 1 3 | Mixolydian without the 4 |
| C7#11 | C · D | 1 3 5 · 9 #11 13 | Lydian dominant |
| C7alt | Gb · Ab | b5 b7 b9 · b13 1 #9 | Altered |
| C7b9 | C · F# | 1 3 5 · #11 b7 b9 | Half-whole |
| C7#5 | C+ · D+ | 1 3 #5 · 9 #11 b7 | Whole tone |
| Cm7b5 | Ebm · Fm | b3 b5 b7 · 11 b13 1 | Locrian #2 |

## One pair, several chords

The six notes don't change; what changes is the bass. **Eb and F** are the Dorian of Cm7, but also Ionian without the 3 over Bbmaj7 and Mixolydian without the 13 over F7. That is why one pair works for a whole ii–V: in Dm7–G7–Cmaj7, **F and G** work for the first two chords (on Dm7 they are b3 and 11; on G7 they are b7 and 1) and resolve to Cmaj7 with G and Am or with D and Em.

On the **altered dominant** the pair is especially useful: bV and bVI major (Gb and Ab in C7) give all the altered tensions, and they sound like two ordinary major chords that anyone can play without thinking "b9, #9, b13".

## How to practise it

1. **Each triad first.** In the example's position, play triad A (filled in) and then B (hollow) separately, saying the degrees over the chord.
2. **Alternate in threes.** One inversion of A, the inversion of B just above it, the next one of A… That is the example's line (*Figure: Up*). Then each group downwards and, finally, A upwards and B downwards.
3. **On two strings.** The triads lie diagonally and the line crosses the neck: useful for getting out of position.
4. **Over the chord.** Turn on *Pedal note* (best with organ or strings) and listen to each pair over its root: the C7alt pair over C sounds very different from the same two triads on their own.
5. **Groups of 2 and 4.** Break the symmetry: two notes of A and two of B, or four and four (the triad with the root repeated on top). The ear stops predicting the next leap.
6. **With rhythm.** Swing eighths, triplets, or three-note groups in eighths (the accent shifts across the bar: that is the charm of the device).
7. **In a tune.** Choose a long chord in a tune from the repertoire (a two-bar maj7, the V7 before the end) and play only the pair there.

## Ideas for variety

- **Two triads, four combinations.** Major-major, major-minor, minor-minor, augmented-augmented: find which ones share notes and which don't.
- **By minor thirds.** With a pair over C7alt, move the pair up a minor third (A and B, then C and D…) and come back to Gb and Ab: the same shape sounds *outside* and the return is clearly heard.
- **Triad melody.** Play only the top notes of each inversion: it is a six-note melody you can sing.
- **Comping.** The two triads, one after the other, as chord voicings over the bass: Gb/C and Ab/C are a very clear C7alt.
