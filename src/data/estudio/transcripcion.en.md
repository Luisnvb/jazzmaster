## The idea

**Transcribing** is working out by ear what someone plays on a recording: a solo, a melody, an accompaniment or a bass line. In jazz it is the traditional way to learn: before method books, musicians learned the language by copying records, just as you learn a language by listening to people who speak it.

Transcribing teaches three things at once that no book gives you together:

- **The ear:** recognising intervals, chords and rhythms in real music, in time and in a mix of instruments.
- **The language:** specific phrases from the masters, with their articulation, their rhythm and their placement over the harmony.
- **The sound:** how a guitarist attacks, slurs and phrases, something that cannot be fully written down.

There is no need to write it on the staff. What matters is **being able to play it and sing it** just like the record. Writing it down is a separate step, useful for analysis, but optional.

## Choosing what to transcribe

Start with something you **really like** and that you **can sing**. If you cannot sing a fragment, you cannot work it out yet.

- **Melodies before solos.** The melody of a standard (from the Repertoire, for example) is the first step: few notes, a clear rhythm and the form of the tune.
- **Slow, melodic solos.** Ballads and medium tempos: Chet Baker, Miles Davis in the fifties, Kenny Burrell, Grant Green, Jim Hall. Clear phrases with space.
- **Then bebop guitarists:** Wes Montgomery, Joe Pass, Pat Martino, George Benson. More notes and faster.
- **Not only guitarists.** Trumpeters, saxophonists and singers give you phrases that sound fresh on the guitar and force you to find your own fingerings.
- **Fragments, not whole solos.** One chorus, eight bars or a single phrase you like. Better one phrase learned well than a whole solo half done.

## The tools

- **Slowing down without changing pitch.** Almost every modern player can do it (dedicated transcription software, audio editors, apps for musicians and the speed setting of video players). At 75 % fast phrases become clear; at 50 % you hear every note.
- **Looping a fragment.** Mark the start and end of a phrase and listen to it over and over without touching anything else.
- **Dropping an octave.** Some tools do it and it helps a lot with very high notes; the other way round, raising an octave helps you hear the bass.
- **EQ.** Cut the lows to hear the guitar or the highs to hear the bass.
- **The guitar next to you,** tuned to the recording. Old records are not always at 440 Hz: if everything sounds "between two notes", tune the guitar to the record.

When you start, slowing down helps; with practice, try to use it less and less. The goal is to hear at real tempo.

## The method, step by step

1. **Listen many times without the guitar.** The whole fragment, until it is in your head. You can listen to it for days before sitting down to work it out.
2. **Sing it.** First with the recording and then without it. If you only hesitate on one note when singing, you know where the problem is. Singing is the proof that you really hear it.
3. **Split it into phrases.** A phrase usually lasts one of the soloist's breaths: between one and four bars. Work phrase by phrase, on a loop.
4. **Find the first note.** Stop the recording on that note, sing it and find it on the guitar. From there, each note is an **interval** from the previous one: up or down? A step or a leap?
5. **Go note by note, singing.** Sing the note, find it, play what you already have from the start of the phrase and add the next one.
6. **Work out the rhythm separately.** Often the notes come before the rhythm. Tap the beat with your foot and sing the phrase on one syllable ("da", "ba") to fix where each note falls: on the beat, off the beat, in triplets.
7. **Play along with the recording.** Slowly first and then in time, over the record, until your phrase and the record's sound as one. Pay attention to articulation: slurs, ghost notes, accents, vibrato, small bends.
8. **Find the original fingering.** The same phrase in another area of the neck sounds different. Ask yourself where the guitarist would have played it: open strings, slides and string skips are clues (see [Fingering and positions](../digitacion/)).

## Clues from the harmony

Knowing which chords are sounding saves a lot of work. Find the chord chart of the tune (or work it out first; see below) and use it:

- **Long notes and notes on strong beats** are usually chord tones or tensions: try the 3, 7, 9 and 13 first.
- **Fast lines** are usually scales, arpeggios or chromatic enclosures. If you hear a stepwise descent, try the chord's scale; if you hear leaps, try the arpeggio (see [Bebop language](../bebop/) and [Chromatic approaches](../aproximaciones/)).
- **At the end of a phrase** there is usually a resolution to a guide tone of the next chord: the 7 going down to the 3 (see [Guide tones](../notas-guia/)).
- **If a note does not fit,** do not force it: it may be a passing note, an altered tension or the soloist playing "outside" on purpose (see [Outside and in](../outside/)).

## Transcribing accompaniments

Working out chords is another exercise, harder at first because several notes sound at once. Go from the outside in:

1. **The bass.** Sing and work out the bass note at each change. It is almost always the root and it tells you where the chord changes.
2. **The form.** Count the bars: 32 in AABA, 12 for a blues? Knowing the form tells you how many chords to look for.
3. **The top voice.** The highest note of the chord is the one heard best. Work it out on each chord: it forms a line.
4. **The quality.** With the bass and the top voice, decide whether the chord is major, minor, dominant or half-diminished. Listen for the 3 (major or minor) and then the 7 (major or minor). [Harmonic ear](../oido-armonico/) trains exactly this.
5. **The voicing.** Finally, find the inner notes and a shape on the guitar that sounds the same: shells, drop 2, fourths…
6. **The rhythm.** How it is comped: Charleston, anticipations, short or long notes (see [Comping](../comping/)).

Jazz progressions repeat a lot: in time you will hear a ii–V–I or a turnaround in one go, without working it out note by note.

## After you have it

Transcribing is half of it; what really teaches you is what you do afterwards.

- **Analyse it.** Write over each note its degree relative to the chord. Where are the guide tones? Which tensions does it use? How does each phrase start and end?
- **Keep phrases.** Choose two or three short phrases you like and learn them separately.
- **Take them to other keys.** Play each phrase in all 12 keys, or at least in the most common keys of the Repertoire. If you cannot move it, it is not yours yet.
- **Use it in other tunes.** Find a ii–V or a similar chord in the Repertoire and put the phrase in. Then change it: another rhythm, another final note, displaced in the bar (see [Motifs and development](../motivos/)).
- **Write it down if it helps.** On the staff, in tab or just with the degrees. A notebook of transcribed phrases is one of the best practice materials there is.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with something too hard.** A fast Pat Martino solo as a first transcription is frustrating. Better eight bars of a ballad.
- **Searching on the guitar without singing first.** Trying frets at random is slow and does not train the ear. First you hear and sing; then you search.
- **Looking at a published transcription.** It is for checking at the end, not for starting: what trains you is the effort of hearing.
- **Paying attention only to the notes.** Rhythm, articulation and sound matter as much or more; a phrase with the exact notes and square rhythm does not sound like jazz.
- **Not playing it again.** What is transcribed and not reviewed is forgotten within a week. Play it every day for a while, as part of your routine.

## How to practise it

1. **One melody a week.** Work out the melody of a tune from the Repertoire from your favourite recording, not from the sheet music. Then compare it with the sheet music: you will see how each performer changes it.
2. **One phrase a day.** Ten or fifteen minutes: a one- to four-bar phrase, sung, worked out, played with the record and taken to another key.
3. **One solo a month.** A whole chorus of a slow solo. Play along with the record until it sounds the same, articulation included.
4. **One accompaniment.** Work out a guitarist's comping (Freddie Green, Jim Hall, Herb Ellis) over a chorus of blues: the bass, the top voice, the quality and the rhythm.
5. **Without slowing down.** From time to time, try to work out a short phrase at real tempo. Measure your progress by how much less you need to slow things down.
