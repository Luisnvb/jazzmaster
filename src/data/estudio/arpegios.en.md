## What an arpeggio is

The notes of a chord played **one at a time**. In improvisation it is the most direct way to make the harmony heard: if each chord's notes sound over it, the chord is heard even if nobody plays it.

A seventh-chord arpeggio has four notes (1, 3, 5, 7) that repeat in every octave. Knowing it all over the neck is not memorising shapes, it is knowing **where each degree is**: where the 3 is in this area, where the 7 is on the next string. ## Three ways through it

Mick Goodrick suggests learning the neck in three directions, and it works just as well with arpeggios:

- **On one string** (the *unitar*): the arpeggio along a single string. You see the real distances between the notes (from 1 to 3, two whole steps; from 3 to 5, a step and a half…) and hear each interval. Choose *One string* in the example.
- **On two strings**: the same arpeggio spread over two neighbouring strings, climbing diagonally. It is the bridge between the single string and the position.
- **In position**: without moving the hand, within five frets. It is the most comfortable for playing fast, but also what most easily turns into a shape played without hearing it. Choose *Position* and go through the twelve positions on the neck.

## How to practise them

1. **Name the degrees.** Play the arpeggio in one position saying the degree of each note (1, 3, 5, 7), not the note name or the finger.
2. **Start from each chord tone.** From the 1, from the 3, from the 5 and from the 7: the arpeggio sounds different depending on where it starts. In the example, *Start on*.
3. **One string, then two.** The same arpeggio on a single string, from the 6th to the 1st, and then on pairs of strings (6-5, 5-4, 4-3…).
4. **Every position.** The twelve positions of an arpeggio, one a day, until you find the 3 and the 7 in any area without thinking.
5. **Change the quality without moving.** In the same position, maj7 → 7 → m7 → m7b5 → °7: notice which notes drop a half step.
6. **Patterns.** The **Patterns** section has sequences over arpeggios (groups of 3 and 4, thirds, approaches…) to play in whatever position you are practising.
7. **In a progression.** In a ii–V–I, play each chord's arpeggio without changing position, and when the chord changes go to the **nearest** note of the new arpeggio instead of going back to the root (that is the idea of [guide tones](../notas-guia/)).

## Arpeggios over other chords

An arpeggio that starts on the 3 (or the 5) of the chord is another seventh-chord arpeggio, and gives you the tensions without looking for them:

| Over | Arpeggio | Notes | Chord degrees |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cmaj7 | Em7 | E G B D | 3 5 7 9 |
| Dm7 | Fmaj7 | F A C E | b3 5 b7 9 |
| G7 | Bm7b5 | B D F A | 3 5 b7 9 |
| G7 | Dm7 | D F A C | 5 b7 9 11 |
| Cm7 | Ebmaj7 | Eb G Bb D | b3 5 b7 9 |

It is the same as [triads over other chords](../triadas/), with one more note. In a ii–V–I, playing Fmaj7 – Bm7b5 – Em7 instead of Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7 immediately sounds more modern.

## Ideas for variety

- **Arpeggio + approach.** Before each note of the arpeggio, a half step below (see [Chromatic approaches](../aproximaciones/)).
- **Arpeggio up, scale down.** Go up the arpeggio and down the chord's scale: it mixes leaps and steps.
- **Rhythm.** The arpeggio in triplets, in displaced groups of 4 eighths or starting off the beat.
- **Two octaves on one string + back in position.** Go up on one string and come down in position: it connects the two ways of seeing the neck.
