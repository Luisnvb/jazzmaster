## The idea

A **motif** is a short idea, three or four notes with a recognisable rhythm, that can be repeated and transformed. A solo built on motifs is heard as a speech: the listener recognises the idea, follows it as it changes and notices when another one appears. A solo that strings together scales and unrelated phrases can have correct notes and still say nothing.

The classic jazz example is Sonny Rollins. Gunther Schuller's analysis of his solo on *Blue 7* (1956) became famous for showing how the whole solo grows out of a few notes of the theme, transformed again and again. But the technique is universal: the first four bars of Beethoven's *Fifth* are three repeated notes and a leap, developed throughout the whole movement.

The important thing is that **developing is neither plain repetition nor changing everything**. Each transformation keeps something (the rhythm, the contour, the intervals) and changes something else, and that is what lets you recognise the idea.

## The transformations

The example above applies these transformations to your motif. Each card plays the original first (in grey) and then the transformation (in colour):

| Transformation | What changes | What is kept |
|---|---|---|
| Repetition | nothing | everything |
| Sequence | moves up one scale degree | rhythm and contour; the intervals adjust to the scale |
| Real transposition | another pitch, the exact intervals | rhythm, contour and intervals |
| Inversion | what went up goes down | rhythm and size of the leaps |
| Retrograde | the order, back to front | notes and durations |
| Displacement | starts one eighth later | notes and rhythm |
| Augmentation | the rhythm, twice as slow | notes and proportions |
| Diminution | the rhythm, twice as fast | notes and proportions |
| Fragmentation | only a piece, repeated | the beginning of the idea |
| Interval expansion | bigger leaps | rhythm and contour |

The most useful when improvising are **repetition**, **sequence**, **transposition** and **displacement**: they can be done in real time without too much thought. Inversion and retrograde belong more to composition, but practising them trains the ear to hear the contour of an idea.

## In a solo

- **Question and answer.** Play the motif (question) and answer it with a transformation (answer): the same idea one degree higher, or with a different ending. Two bars each.
- **Three times and change.** Repeat the idea twice and change its ending the third time. It is the form of many blues melodies (AAB) and it builds expectation.
- **Follow the chords.** In a ii–V–I, play the motif on the ii, transpose it to the V (a 4th up, like the chord) and resolve it on the I. If the real transposition clashes with a chord, adjust the clashing note (that is already a sequence).
- **Fixed rhythm, new notes.** Keep the motif's rhythm and change the notes to those of the chord that is sounding. The ear recognises rhythm before pitch.
- **Displace against the bar.** Repeat the same idea starting one eighth later each time: the motif crosses the bar line and creates rhythmic tension without changing notes.

## How to practise it

1. **One motif, one chord.** With a fixed background chord ("With background chord"), invent a three-note motif and play it with each transformation in the example. First listen to them; then play them yourself without looking.
2. **Twelve bars with a single idea.** Improvise over a blues using only one motif and its transformations. Playing anything that doesn't come from it is forbidden.
3. **Sing and play.** Sing a motif, play it and sing the answer before playing it. If you can't sing it, it is too complicated.
4. **Learn motifs from the tunes.** The melodies in the Repertoire are full of motifs: the cell of *Footprints*, the one in *Cantaloupe Island*. Write them into the editor and see which transformations the tune itself uses.
5. **Record and listen.** In a recording of your solo, count how many different ideas there are. If there are too many, next time develop fewer ideas for longer.

## Ideas for variety

- **Combine transformations:** a displaced sequence, an augmented inversion. The example does one at a time; you can chain them.
- **Change the scale:** the same motif in Dorian, Mixolydian or harmonic minor changes character without changing shape.
- **Rhythmic motifs on one note:** a rhythm on a single pitch is a motif. Develop it by changing only the rhythm (see [Rhythm](../ritmo/)).
- **From motif to line:** join the motif to a bebop line (see [Bebop language](../bebop/)): the motif gives the identity and the line leads to the next chord.
