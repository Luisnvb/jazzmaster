## What reharmonising is

**Reharmonising** is replacing the chords of a progression (or of a melody) with others that have the same function or lead to the same place by another route. The melody and the form stay the same; the colour and movement underneath change.

It happens in comping, when improvising (thinking of chords that are not sounding) and when arranging. The foundation is [functional harmony](../armonia/): if you know each chord's function, you know what can replace it.

Two rules for it to work:

- **The melody rules.** The new chord has to contain the melody note (as a chord tone or a tension that sounds good). If it clashes, it won't do.
- **The destination stays.** Reharmonising changes the route, not where you arrive: the important cadences (the end of each section) usually stay.

## Techniques

Those in the example above, from the subtlest to the most striking:

| Technique | Example in C | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| Diatonic substitution | C – C → C – Am7 | Another chord with the same function (they share notes). |
| Modal interchange | F → Fm7 · Dm7 – G7 → Dø7 – G7 | Chords borrowed from the minor mode: minor subdominant. |
| Chain of dominants | Em7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7 → E7 – A7 – D7 – G7 | Chords that rise a fourth become dominants. |
| Secondary dominants | C – Dm7 → C A7 – Dm7 | The dominant of the next chord, in the second half of the bar. |
| Secondary ii–Vs | C – F → C Gm7 C7 – F | The ii–V of the next chord. |
| Tritone substitution | Dm7 – G7 – C → Dm7 – Db7 – C | Each dominant replaced by the one a tritone away. Chromatic bass. |
| Passing diminished | C – Dm7 → C C#°7 – Dm7 | A °7 links two chords a whole step apart. |

They can be combined: a chain of dominants with tritone substitution gives a bass that falls by half steps (E7 – A7 – D7 – G7 → Bb7 – Eb7 – Ab7 – Db7 if all are substituted, or alternating some yes and some no).

## How to hear the changes

Always compare with the original: the example above has a button for each version. As you listen, pay attention to:

- **The bass.** Tritone substitution and passing diminished chords make it chromatic; the chain of dominants keeps it moving in fourths.
- **The tension.** Secondary dominants and ii–Vs add movement and push towards the next chord; modal interchange darkens.
- **The density.** More chords isn't always better. A good reharmonisation usually changes two or three spots, not all of them.

## How to practise it

1. **One technique at a time.** On a short progression (ii–V–I, turnaround), turn on a single technique, listen to it against the original and play it on the guitar with [shell voicings](../shells/) or [drop 2](../drop2/).
2. **Combine two.** Secondary dominants + tritone substitution, or secondary ii–Vs + diminished chords. Find the one you like best and learn the result by heart.
3. **With the melody.** Play the melody of a simple standard and, underneath, the reharmonisation. Check in every bar that the melody note fits in the new chord.
4. **Reharmonise a standard.** Choose a tune from the Repertoire and change only the bars of long tonic (two bars of the same chord): that is where it shows most and there is least risk.
5. **Improvise over the reharmonisation.** Even if the rest of the band plays the original harmony, playing over the new chords (a ii–V where there is a single chord, a subV where there is a V7) gives the improvisation controlled tension.
6. **In every key.** The techniques don't depend on the key: practise each one in all twelve, like the ii–V–I.

## Ideas for variety

- **Reharmonising the tonic.** A four-bar tonic can become I – VI7 – IIm7 – V7 (a turnaround) or I – bIIImaj7 – bVImaj7 – bIImaj7 (parallel maj7 chords).
- **Pedal bass.** Keep the bass note and change the chords above it: the same chord over a different bass is already another harmony.
- **Coltrane.** The *Giant Steps* progression divides the octave into three major thirds: an extreme exercise in secondary dominants.
