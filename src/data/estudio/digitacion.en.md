## The idea

A scale can be played in many ways on the guitar: the same note is in several places on the neck. **Fingering** is the decision about which string, which fret and which finger plays each note. It is not a technical detail: it decides which phrases come naturally, how much the hand moves and which area of the neck you think in.

There are two systems worth knowing, because they complement each other:

- **Fixed position:** the hand stays still and each finger takes care of one fret. It is the system of reading and of classic jazz guitar (Joe Pass, Jim Hall, the Leavitt method).
- **Three notes per string:** each string carries three consecutive scale notes and the hand moves up diagonally. It is the system of fast, long lines (Pat Martino, Frank Gambale, fusion).

In the exercise above you choose a scale and two fingerings, **A** and **B**, one above the other on necks of the same length. Under each one are its figures: how many frets it spans, how many notes there are on each string, how many stretches and how many position shifts. Press "Play" to hear each one (up and down) or "A and B in a row" to compare them.

## The 12 positions

A **position** is where the hand sits: it is named after the fret where the **index finger** falls. In position 5, the index plays fret 5, the middle finger 6, the ring finger 7 and the little finger 8. **One finger per fret.**

Any seven-note scale fits in any position: there are **12 positions**, one per fret from 1 to 12 (13 repeats 1 an octave higher). In each one the scale spans four frets, and some notes fall just outside, one fret below or above the hand. Those are played with a **stretch**:

- **The little finger forward** (position fret + 4).
- **The index finger back** (position fret − 1).

The hand does not move: one finger just opens out and comes back. Some positions need none (C major in position 7) and others one or two. In the exercise, stretches have a dashed border.

Knowing the scale in all 12 positions means being able to play **anywhere** on the neck without moving: if the tune is in Bb and you are in position 3, there is no need to look for "the Bb shape"; the scale is right there under your fingers. That is what lets you read, comp and improvise without losing your place.

## Three notes per string

In this system each string carries exactly **three notes** of the scale, in a row. Since a seven-note scale has seven possible starting notes, you get **seven shapes**, one per scale degree on the 6th string.

Advantages:

- **The same gesture on every string**, which makes picking regular (three strokes per string; see *Picking technique*) and helps legato.
- **Two octaves and a 3rd** without changing shape: 18 notes in a row.
- **Repeatable patterns:** sequences of 3 and 6 notes always fall the same way.

Drawbacks:

- **Frequent stretches:** when the three notes are a whole step apart (whole–whole), the hand opens over five frets (fingers 1, 2 and 4).
- **The hand moves up diagonally:** between the 3rd and 2nd strings (which are a major 3rd apart, not a 4th) and on other strings, the shape shifts one or two frets. These are **position shifts** hidden inside the shape; in the exercise they are marked with a thick border.

The fingers are always the index on the first note and the little finger on the last; the middle note is played by the **middle finger** if it is closer to the first (or equally close) and by the **ring finger** if it is closer to the last.

## How to shift position

No position reaches the whole neck: sooner or later the hand has to move. The most common ways:

- **Slide a finger:** the same finger plays two consecutive notes on the same string and carries the hand to the new place. It is audible: it is used as an expressive device.
- **Shift on the half step:** use a half step in the scale (E–F, B–C in C major) so the index takes the little finger's fret or vice versa. It is the cleanest shift, almost inaudible.
- **Shift by stretching:** open a finger towards the new position and let the hand follow it.
- **Open string:** an open note gives you time to move the hand without breaking the line.
- **Shift on a rest:** on a rest or a long note, the hand resettles without hurry. In jazz the phrase rules: choose the fingering so the shifts fall on the rests.

A useful rule: **shift up leading with the index and down leading with the little finger** (the finger at the front of the movement). That way the hand arrives in the new position with the fingers already in place.

## Which to choose

There is no better system; each is good for something:

- **Fixed position** for reading, for comping with chords and scales in the same area, for thinking in degrees and for playing with little movement.
- **Three notes per string** for long, fast lines, for regular sequences and for covering the neck.
- **A mix:** most jazz guitarists think in positions and use three notes per string occasionally, where the line calls for it.

Compare in the exercise a fixed position (A) with the three-notes-per-string shape that starts in the same area (B): you will see the same notes distributed differently.

## The modes

The modes of the major scale (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian…) do not need new fingerings: they are **the same scale starting on another degree**. D Dorian is C major from the 2, G Mixolydian is C major from the 5. In three notes per string, each "From the …" shape is in practice a mode; in fixed position, the mode only changes where the tonic the ear leans on is.

The same goes for melodic minor (Lydian dominant from the 4, altered from the 7) and harmonic minor (Phrygian dominant from the 5).

## How to practise it

1. **One position a day.** Choose a position (A) and play the scale up and down, slowly, saying the degrees. Then change key without moving the hand: position 5 works for C, F, Bb, G, D…
2. **The 12 positions of one scale.** In the same key, go through positions 1 to 12. Notice which ones need stretches and why.
3. **Compare.** Put a position in A and in B the three-notes-per-string shape that starts nearby. Play both in a row and listen to whether the second sounds more connected.
4. **Shifts on the half step.** Play the scale over two octaves starting in position 2 and ending in 7, shifting position only on a half step.
5. **Sequences.** Take the sequences from the Scales page (thirds, groups of 3 and 4) into a fixed position and a three-notes-per-string shape: that is how you feel what each system is for.
6. **With a tune.** Play the melody of a standard from the Repertoire in three different positions. Choose the one that moves least and write down the fingering.
