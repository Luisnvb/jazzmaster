A routine in two blocks. **A** works on a single chord in depth: the ear and the map of the neck. **B** takes that into a tune from the repertoire without leaving one position. Use the draw above so you do not always pick the comfortable option.

**Typical session (40–50 min):** 5 min warm-up with the note of the day (A1) · 15–20 min on one exercise from A · 20 min of B with a tune · 5 min of free playing, with no goal.

## Principles

Inspired by Mick Goodrick's way of teaching (*The Advancing Guitarist*) and by his work on voice leading (*Mr. Goodchord's Almanac of Guitar Voice-Leading*).

- **The neck has three directions.** Along one string (horizontal), across the strings without moving the hand (vertical, in position) and combining both (diagonal). Goodrick suggests starting with a single string, the *unitar*: on one string intervals are *seen* as distances, without the trap of learned shapes.
- **Limit in order to discover.** Few notes, one string, one position, one rhythm. The smaller the material, the more you have to listen and invent with it. The restriction is the tool, not a preliminary stage.
- **Sound before shape.** Sing what you are going to play. If you cannot sing it, you do not hear it yet.
- **Always name two things:** the note (Eb) and its function in the chord (b3, 7, 9…).
- **First without tempo, then with the metronome.** Explore in free time until it comes out without hesitation; then set the metronome on 2 and 4 at a slow tempo.
- **Visualise without the guitar.** Go over in your head where the notes are: it is real practice and you can do it anywhere.

## A. One note and one quality

Choose a root and a quality (major, minor, 7, m7b5…) and stay with it for the whole session. By the end you should be able to find any chord tone anywhere on the neck and hear it before you play it.

First listen to the chord and its arpeggio in **Chords** (strum and arpeggio buttons) to get the sound in your head.

### A1. Recognising the note

- Play the root on all six strings, from the 6th to the 1st and back, saying its name. Then all its octaves along each string.
- **One string (unitar):** go up a single string playing only the chord tones, from low to high. Then the other strings.
- **Ear:** sing the note before playing it and check. Then sing the 3rd and the 5th above it.
- **Reading speed:** metronome at 60, one note per beat, each time on a different string (6th, 3rd, 5th, 1st…). Raise the tempo when you stop missing.

### A2. Close triads

The three chord tones within one octave, on **groups of three adjacent strings**: 6-5-4, 5-4-3, 4-3-2 and 3-2-1. Each group has three positions: root position, 1st and 2nd inversion.

- **Vertical (in position):** without moving the hand, find the triad on the four string groups.
- **Horizontal (along the neck):** on a single string group, go up through the three inversions to the octave and back.
- **String-by-string arpeggio:** one triad note per string, each note on the string next to the previous one. Go up from the 6th to the 1st and back down, changing inversion when needed so you never skip a string.
- **Listen to the top voice:** say which degree is on the highest note (1, 3 or 5). It is the one heard as the melody.
- **Goodrick:** play the diatonic triads of the scale (I, ii, iii, IV…) on one string group and move from one to the next with the smallest possible movement in each voice. More in [Triads](../triadas/).

### A3. Arpeggio (four-note chord)

- 1-3-5-7 in the chosen position, two octaves, from low to high and back.
- **Start on each chord tone:** from the 3, from the 5, from the 7. It changes the sound a lot.
- **On one string and on two adjacent strings:** the same arpeggio along the neck.
- **Combinations:** go up the arpeggio and down the scale; the arpeggio in thirds (1-3, 3-5, 5-7…).
- Connect it with **Chords**: compare the arpeggio with the voicings of that quality and find each degree in the diagram.
- The fretboard map in [Arpeggios](../arpegios/) shows every chord tone with its degree, by position or by string.

### A4. Open triads

The middle note of the close triad goes up an octave: the chord opens up and spans more than an octave, with a wider sound, like a string section. They are played on **string groups with one string skipped**: 6-4-3, 5-3-2 and 4-2-1 (also 6-5-3, 5-4-2 and 4-3-1).

- The same three inversions as in A2, on each group.
- Alternate close and open versions of the same inversion to hear the difference.
- Very useful for comping: the low note acts as the bass and the two high ones as a pad.

### A5. Pentatonic

Which pentatonics fit each quality (from the root of the chord):

| Quality | Pentatonic | Degrees it gives |
|---|---|---|
| maj7 | major from the 1 · major from the 5 | 1 2 3 5 6 · 5 6 7 9 3 |
| m7 | minor from the 1 · minor from the 2 | 1 b3 4 5 b7 · 9 11 5 13 1 |
| 7 | major from the 1 · minor from the 5 | 1 9 3 5 13 · 5 b7 1 9 11 |
| m7b5 | minor with b5 from the 1 | 1 b3 4 b5 b7 |

- Play it in the chosen position, on a single string and spread over two strings.
- Mark in your head which notes are chord tones and which are tensions, and end your phrases on chord tones.

### A6. Scale shape

The scale for the quality: Ionian (maj7), Dorian (m7), Mixolydian (7), Locrian (m7b5)… See the **Scales** section.

- **Goodrick, in order:** first on one string (you see the whole and half steps), then on two adjacent strings, finally in position, without moving the hand.
- Within the shape, **play only the chord tones** and then only the tensions (9, 11, 13). It is the bridge between the arpeggio and the scale.
- Change position without changing root: the same scale with another fingering is another map of the same sound.
- The fretboard map in [Scales on the neck](../escalas/) marks the chord tones within the scale, by position or by string.

## B. Repertoire in one position

Choose a tune from the **Repertoire** and a position on the neck (a starting fret and the next 4–5). Do not leave that area until the last step. Loop the tune at a comfortable tempo and work through the list below; each step is a day's work, there is no need to do them all in one session.

For each chord:

1. **Identify every note of the triad or four-note chord** in the position: where the 1, the 3, the 5 and the 7 are. Say the name and the degree.
2. **Sing before you play.** Sing the root of each chord following the harmony; then the 3; then the whole arpeggio. Play only to check.
3. **Restrict the improvisation** to a few chord tones:
   - only 1 and 5 · only 3 and 7 · a single note per chord
   - a fixed rhythm (for example, only quarter notes, or a pattern from [Rhythm](../ritmo/)) and then free
   - Goodrick: there is more music in two notes than it seems. Exhaust the limitation before moving on to the next one.
4. **Connect the guide tones (3 and 7)** of each chord with those of the next by the nearest note. In ii–V–Is the 7 of one chord goes down a half step to the 3 of the next and the 3 stays as the 7: two voices that hardly move and outline the whole harmony. Play the guide-tone line on its own and then use it as the skeleton of your improvisation. More in [Guide tones](../notas-guia/).
5. **Chain inversions,** close or open, moving each voice as little as possible.
   - Goodrick organises these progressions in **cycles** according to how the root moves: cycle 4 (up a fourth: ii–V–I, the most common in jazz), cycle 2 (by step), cycle 3 (by thirds), cycle 5, cycle 6… Practise the progression in its own cycle and notice which voices stay still. More in [Goodrick cycles](../ciclos/).
   - Pay attention to the top voice: on its own it can be the melody of the accompaniment.
6. **Add chromaticism on the weak beats** (2 and 4, or on the off-beat eighths): chromatic passing notes or half-step approaches, from above or below, landing on a chord tone on the strong beat (1 and 3). More in [Chromatic approaches](../aproximaciones/).
7. **Add scale notes** for each chord to fill in between chord tones, and try the tensions (9, 11, 13) as long notes.
8. **Change position** and repeat from step 1. Then connect two positions in the same chorus. Finally, play the tune on a single string (unitar): it forces you to hear every interval.

## Ideas for variety

- **Goodrick cycles with your chord:** take the diatonic progression of the key and go through it in cycle 4, cycle 2 and cycle 3 with triads on one string group.
- **One interval a day:** improvise over the tune using only thirds, or only fourths, between consecutive notes.
- **Two-bar phrases and answer:** play two bars and leave two in silence, singing the answer.
- **Imitation:** sing a short phrase and play it in three different positions.
- **Limited register:** improvise only on the two top strings, or only on the three lowest.
