## What they are

The **3** and the **7** of each chord. They are the two notes that say which chord it is:

- the 3 decides whether it is major or minor (3 or b3);
- the 7 decides whether it is maj7, 7 or m7 (7 or b7), and in the diminished chord it is bb7.

The root is usually played by the bass and the 5 hardly changes the colour (it is the same in maj7, 7 and m7), so with just two notes per chord you hear the whole harmony. In sixth chords (6, m6) the 6 plays the role of the 7, and in sus chords the 4 plays the role of the 3.

## How they move

If each guide tone goes to the nearest one in the next chord, two lines appear that barely move: notes that stay put and steps of a half or whole step. That is what you see in the example above.

**The ii–V–I rule** (and of all movement by fourths, Goodrick's cycle 4):

| | Dm7 | G7 | Cmaj7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top voice | C (b7) | B (3) ↓½ | B (7) = |
| Bottom voice | F (b3) | F (b7) = | E (3) ↓½ |

- The **7** of one chord falls a half step and becomes the **3** of the next.
- The **3** stays still and becomes the **7** of the next.
- So the 3 and the 7 take turns between the two lines: one chord has the 7 on top and the next the 3. That is why the example lets you choose what the top voice starts with: you get two different journeys through the same progression.

**The dominant's tritone.** In G7 the 3 (B) and the 7 (F) form a tritone, the interval that wants to resolve. Db7 has the same two notes (F and Cb = B) with the roles swapped: that is why the tritone substitution works.

**Triads.** Without a 7, the second voice is the root or the 5 (whichever is closer). In triad progressions, such as classical cadences, the 3 sometimes has to leap: the lines are less smooth than with seventh chords.

## How to practise them

1. **Listen and sing.** Loop the progression with the bass and a sustained sound. Sing the top line; then the bottom one. Then remove the bass and try to hear the harmony with just the two lines.
2. **Two notes on the guitar.** Play the guide tones on a pair of strings: 3-2 or 4-3 (for example, Dm7 with F on the 4th and C on the 3rd). Without moving your hand more than one fret per change.
3. **Shell voicings.** Add the root on the 6th or 5th string: root + 3 + 7 (or root + 7 + 3). These are the three-note chords of classic jazz comping; alternate the two types so the hand hardly moves. More in [Shell voicings](../shells/).
4. **One guide tone per chord.** Improvise playing only one of the two lines, one note per chord on beat 1. Then two notes per chord (3 and 7 in whichever order you like).
5. **Land on the guide tone.** Improvise freely but land on the guide tone on beat 1 of each chord:
   - from a half step below or above (chromatic approach, on beat 4 of the previous bar);
   - surrounding it: the note above and the note below before landing (*enclosure*);
   - from the scale, stepping down or up.
6. **Add colour on top.** Play the guide-tone line in the low voice and put a tension on top (9 or 13) that also moves little: three voices with a big-band sound.
7. **In your tunes.** Work out the guide-tone lines of each standard in the repertoire before improvising on it. Write them down or sing them: they are the skeleton of the tune.

## Ideas for variety

- **Guide line in another register.** The same progression with the guide tones on strings 2-1 and then on 5-4.
- **Rhythm.** Play the two notes in a Charleston rhythm (beat 1 and the "&" of 2) or anticipated on the "&" of 4, as in comping.
- **Only the top line as a melody.** Play it alone, with rhythm, and repeat it until it sounds like a phrase.
- **Guide tones + cycles.** Go through cycle 4 on the **Goodrick cycles** page playing only the guide tones.
